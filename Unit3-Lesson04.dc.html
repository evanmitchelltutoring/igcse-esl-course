The Energy Trap

Read · then read again

Walk into almost any corner shop after school and you will see them: brightly coloured cans promising instant energy, sharper focus and better performance. Energy drinks have become one of the most popular products among teenagers, yet many young people have no idea what they are actually drinking.

A single large can can contain as much caffeine as two cups of strong coffee, along with several spoonfuls of sugar. In small amounts, caffeine is harmless for most adults. The problem is the dose. Health experts recommend that teenagers consume no more than one hundred milligrams of caffeine a day; some energy drinks contain three times that in a single serving.

The promised 'energy' is also misleading. The drinks do not create energy; they borrow it. Caffeine and sugar produce a short, sharp lift, usually followed an hour or two later by a crash that leaves the drinker more tired, and often hungrier, than before. Students who rely on them during exam season frequently report poorer sleep and difficulty concentrating — the very opposite of what they were hoping for.

Some countries have responded with rules. In 2024, a number of supermarkets across the United Kingdom stopped selling the strongest energy drinks to anyone under sixteen, even before a law required it. Schools in several cities have removed them from vending machines entirely, replacing them with water stations and chilled fruit.

There are simpler alternatives for a genuine lift. Drinking water is the cheapest: even mild dehydration can cause tiredness and headaches that are easily mistaken for hunger. A handful of nuts, a banana, or a short walk outside will all raise alertness without the later crash.

None of this means the occasional can is dangerous. The advice from nutritionists is about awareness, not fear: read the label, know your limit, and remember that real, lasting energy comes from sleep, food and movement — not from a can.

1. As much as two cups of strong coffee (plus several spoonfuls of sugar).
2. No more than 100 mg a day.

Answer · mark your own

Five short extracts — the answers

Extract 1 — A. "Barely — just a quick biscuit. I crashed by second period."
Extract 2 — B. "Just walk and do some squats at home. You won't keep paying for the gym anyway."
Extract 3 — B. "Try leaving your phone in another room. Sounds silly, but it worked for me."

Notice: none of the correct options used the speaker's exact words — you matched the meaning.
