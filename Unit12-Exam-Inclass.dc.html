Insert — the text

Read the whole text once before you begin. Paragraphs are numbered.

1  Nobody planned to spend a night on Karren Island. We had rented the small sailing boat for a single afternoon, and by four o'clock we were supposed to be back at the harbour, returning the keys and buying ice creams before the drive home. Instead, at four o'clock, my brother Theo and I were standing on a strip of grey sand, watching our boat drift further out to sea with every wave.

2  It had been my idea to land on the island "just for ten minutes", to see the old lighthouse close up. Theo had argued against it — he always did — but I had already jumped out into the shallow water, and rather than watch me explore alone, he had followed, dragging the boat up onto the sand as he came. Neither of us had tied it properly. A rope that looks secure on wet sand is not secure at all, and the tide, when it turns, does not ask permission.

3  By the time we noticed, the boat was a distant white speck, and shouting at it achieved nothing except making us hoarse. There was no phone signal on the island, no other boats in sight, and the mainland was a smudge of grey on the horizon, much too far to swim.

4  "We are not going to panic," Theo said, in a voice that suggested he very much wanted to. He was right, though. Panic would not bring the boat back, and it would not build us anywhere to shelter before dark. We searched the island — which took less than twenty minutes, it was so small — and found only the ruined lighthouse, roofless but with three solid walls, and a scatter of dry driftwood above the tideline.

5  We spent the afternoon building a fire, more for the comfort of doing something useful than because we expected anyone to see it. We had one bottle of water between us and a bag of crackers, which we rationed without needing to discuss it — some agreements do not need words. When the sun went down, we sat inside the broken lighthouse walls, close to the fire, and Theo, of all things, started telling jokes. Bad ones. I had never been so glad of them.

6  A fishing boat found us not long after dawn, drawn in by the thin thread of smoke still rising from our fire. The fisherman was kind and did not lecture us, which somehow made me feel worse about the whole thing. I have never again left anything I care about tied by a rope I have not checked twice. And Theo, to this day, has never once let me forget whose idea the ten minutes was.

Marking — how your answers are judged
BandWhat it looks like
StrongAll factual answers correct and precise; summary in your own words, points only, within the word count. For craft questions, you identify the writer's choice and support it with the exact words that carry it, explaining their effect on the reader.
SecureMost factual answers correct; summary covers the main points with some own wording. You show the writer creates feeling and point to some evidence, though the explanation of how the words work may be brief.

D1 summary (6): reward, in own words — the brothers landed on the island and failed to tie the boat, which drifted away on the tide, leaving them stranded with no signal. They stayed calm, sheltered in the ruined lighthouse, built a fire, rationed their water and kept each other's spirits up until a fishing boat, guided by the smoke, rescued them at dawn. Up to 4 for content, up to 2 for own-words expression within 50–70 words. Penalise lifted sentences and over-length.
E1 (2) Giving the tide a will of its own ("does not ask permission") makes it sound powerful and indifferent — nature won't wait for human carelessness; it stresses that the mistake had unforgiving consequences.
E2 (2) The bad jokes show the brothers keeping each other's morale up — humour, not heroics, is how they endure the frightening night; "so glad of them" reveals how much comfort the ordinary company gave.
E3 (4) Yes — he takes the blame. Reward two supported points, e.g. "It had been my idea to land" and "the fisherman … did not lecture us, which somehow made me feel worse", plus the closing promise never again to trust "a rope I have not checked twice."
