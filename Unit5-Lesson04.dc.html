The Thing a Machine Cannot Feel

by a working illustrator

I have spent twenty years learning to draw. Twenty years of failed sketches, aching hands, and quiet mornings spent chasing a line that finally felt right. So when people tell me a machine can now make art in ten seconds, I understand why they are excited. The images are often beautiful — but beauty was never the whole point.

Here is what those ten-second pictures are missing. When I draw my grandmother's hands, I am not simply arranging shapes that look like hands. I am remembering the smell of her kitchen, the way she held a pencil, the afternoon she taught me to shade. Every deliberate choice — this shadow, that gap, the slight tremble in the line — carries a meaning that I put there on purpose. The drawing is not just a picture of her hands. It is what I feel about her hands.

A machine has no grandmother. It has no afternoon to remember. It can study a million drawings and learn how a hand is shaped. It can even mimic the look of grief or joy. But it has never felt either. Although the result may move you, the machine intended nothing by it. The feeling you see was not placed there — you brought it yourself.

People sometimes call my worry old-fashioned. Perhaps it is. New tools have always frightened the people they replace, and I will not pretend the camera did not terrify painters once. But there is a difference between a tool that helps a human express something and a tool that removes the human altogether. A camera still needs an eye behind it — a reason to press the button at that exact moment.

This is why the word "soulless" keeps returning to me. I do not mean the pictures are ugly; many are stunning. I mean they come from nowhere — answers to a question no one was really asking, made by something that wanted nothing, remembered nothing, and risked nothing. So make your beautiful pictures in ten seconds, if you like. I will keep my twenty years.
