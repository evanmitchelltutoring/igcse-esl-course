Coming Home Changed: The Shock Nobody Warns You About

Read · then read again

Most travellers prepare for culture shock — the disorientation of arriving somewhere new. Far fewer expect its mirror image: reverse culture shock, the strange difficulty of returning home.

Researchers describe it in stages. At first, returnees feel relief and excitement at familiar faces and food. This 'honeymoon' fades within weeks. A second stage often brings frustration: home has carried on without them, friends have moved on, and stories from abroad are met with polite but brief interest. Returnees may feel that they have changed while everything they left behind has stayed the same — or worse, changed in ways they no longer recognise. The feeling is disorienting precisely because it is unexpected: the traveller braced for a foreign country, not for a strange version of their own.

A third stage is gradual readjustment. Returnees learn to hold two things at once: the person they were before and the person they became away. Many report that they never fully return to who they were. They notice things about home they once took for granted, and they carry habits, tastes and questions picked up elsewhere.

Psychologists suggest ways to ease the transition. Keeping in contact with friends made abroad helps the experience feel real rather than imagined. Joining a returnee group or online community reduces the sense of being alone with a story no one wants to hear. And keeping one or two habits from the host country — a food, a phrase, a routine — lets the person they were abroad stay alive in the person they are at home.

The length of adjustment varies. As a rough guide, the deeper the immersion and the longer the time away, the longer the readjustment tends to take. Knowing this in advance does not remove the strangeness — but it does make it easier to name.

• keep contact with friends abroad

How long it takes
• varies person to person
• deeper immersion / longer away → longer readjustment
