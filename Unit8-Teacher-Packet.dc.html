The reading texts
Why do we look up?
Reading text

1For as long as people have existed, they have gazed at the night sky and wondered what lies beyond it. Long before rockets or telescopes, farmers used the stars to mark the seasons and sailors used them to find their way home. Yet our interest in space has never been purely practical. Something about the darkness overhead has always stirred a deep sense of wonder — a feeling that there is more to discover than we can ever see from the ground.

2That curiosity eventually carried human beings off the planet altogether. The first Moon landing in 1969 was a milestone that millions watched on television, holding their breath as a person stepped onto another world for the first time. For many who saw it, the moment was less about science than about inspiration: if humans could reach the Moon, what else might they achieve? Teachers reported that children who had never thought about engineering suddenly wanted to build spacecraft. A whole generation grew up aspiring to careers that had barely existed a decade earlier.

3Of course, curiosity is not the only reason nations spend enormous sums on space. During the twentieth century, sending astronauts beyond Earth became a way of demonstrating power. A successful launch brought a country prestige, suggesting to the world that its scientists and engineers were among the best. Some historians argue that the race to the Moon was driven as much by competition between rival nations as by any genuine wish to expand human knowledge. The ambition was real, but so was the desire to be seen as a leader.

4Today the picture is more complicated. Governments still run space programmes, but private companies now design and launch their own rockets, sometimes carrying paying passengers. Critics complain that this turns a noble adventure into a business, and that the wonder of the early years has been replaced by advertising. Supporters reply that competition has made spaceflight cheaper and that lower costs will eventually allow far more ordinary people to take part.

5Whatever one thinks of these changes, the underlying impulse seems unlikely to fade. Space exploration is expensive, dangerous and often disappointing; missions fail, budgets are cut, and progress can feel painfully slow. And yet, again and again, people return to the same questions. Are we alone? Could we live somewhere else? What is out there, just beyond the reach of our most powerful instruments?

6Perhaps the honest answer is that we explore space for the same reason our ancestors studied the stars: not only because it is useful, but because we cannot help ourselves. The pioneering spirit that once pushed explorers across oceans now points upward. Long after the headlines fade, that restless curiosity — humanity's oldest and most stubborn ambition — will still be looking up.

Counting the cost
Reading text

1Every rocket that climbs into the sky carries an invisible price tag. Space programmes are among the most expensive projects any government can undertake, and the question of whether that expenditure is justified has been argued for decades.

2The most obvious cost is money. A single crewed mission can swallow billions, and the budget for a long-term project such as a Mars base would stretch across many years and many governments. Critics point to what economists call the opportunity cost: every sum allocated to a launch pad is a sum not spent on hospitals, schools or clean water. When public money is involved, they argue, the taxpayer has a right to ask whether such spending is wise — or whether it simply squanders resources that are urgently needed at home.

3Supporters answer that the figures are smaller than they appear. In most countries, space agencies receive only a tiny fraction of national budgets, far less than is spent on defence. They also argue that the money is not fired into the sky and lost; it is paid to engineers, factories and universities on Earth, creating skilled jobs and knowledge that remain long after a mission ends.

4Money, however, is not the only cost. Spaceflight is dangerous. Astronauts face hazards that most workers never imagine: extreme radiation, the failure of life-support systems, and months of isolation that can damage mental as well as physical health. The history of exploration is marked by tragic setbacks in which crews have lost their lives. Each such disaster forces agencies to pause, investigate and rebuild — a process that is both costly and slow.

5There is also a cost that is easy to overlook: the environment. Rocket launches release gases high into the atmosphere, and the growing number of satellites has filled orbit with debris that threatens future missions. As launches become more frequent, some scientists question whether the current pace is sustainable at all.

6None of this means that the spending is automatically wrong. Many worthwhile activities are expensive and risky. But it does mean that the case for space cannot rest on wonder alone. Those who defend it must show that the benefits — to science, to industry, and to ordinary life — are real enough to justify the bills, the dangers and the damage. That is a harder argument to win than simply pointing at the stars.

What space gives back
Reading text

1When people argue that space exploration is a waste of money, supporters often reply with a single word: spin-offs. A spin-off is a useful product or technology that was developed for space but ends up benefiting ordinary life on Earth — and the list is longer than most people realise.

2The clearest examples come from the sky itself. Thousands of satellites now orbit the planet, and we depend on them every day. Weather forecasts that once relied on guesswork are now built from satellite images, giving farmers and emergency services days of warning before a storm. Satellite navigation guides ships, aircraft and the phone in almost every pocket. Without this orbiting infrastructure, much of modern communication would simply stop.

3Other benefits are less visible but just as real. The harsh demands of spaceflight — where equipment must be light, reliable and able to survive without repair — have driven innovation in materials, medicine and energy. Technologies first designed to keep astronauts alive have found applications in hospitals, from water-purification systems to better ways of monitoring patients. Several everyday objects, including scratch-resistant lenses and certain cordless tools, began life as a by-product of space research rather than as its goal.

4Supporters argue that this pattern is not accidental. Difficult problems, they say, force engineers to think in new ways, and the breakthroughs that result rarely stay locked inside the space programme. A government that funds exploration, in this view, is really funding a kind of advanced research laboratory whose discoveries spread across the whole economy.

5Critics are not fully convinced. They accept that spin-offs exist, but they question whether space is the cheapest way to produce them. If the real goal is better medicine or cleaner energy, they ask, why not fund medicine and energy directly, rather than hoping for useful accidents along the way? The benefits, they argue, are used after the event to justify spending that was decided for other reasons.

6What is clear is that space exploration does not happen in isolation from life on Earth. The satellites overhead, the forecasts on the news and the tools in our hospitals are reminders that looking outward has, again and again, changed the way we live down here.

The next horizon
Reading text

1Stand outside on a clear night and look up. That scatter of cold light has pulled at human beings for a hundred thousand years, and now, for the first time, the door to it is creaking open.

2We are told this is the dawn of a new age. Rockets that once cost a fortune and burned up after a single flight now land themselves, upright, like dancers taking a bow. Companies speak of cities on Mars as casually as their grandparents spoke of crossing an ocean. The message is everywhere, glossy and confident: the future is up there, and it is almost within reach.

3But step back from the advertising, and a harder question waits. Who, exactly, is this future for?

4For now, a seat beyond the atmosphere costs more than most people will earn in a lifetime. The 'space for everyone' we are promised looks, on closer inspection, remarkably like space for a fortunate few — a glittering departure lounge with a velvet rope, and the rest of us pressed against the glass. The same restless energy that could lift humanity outward could just as easily carry our oldest habits with it: our greed, our carelessness, our talent for leaving others behind.

5And yet it would be a thin, grey kind of caution that refused to dream at all. There is something in us that needs the horizon. Strip it away and we shrink. The child who watches a rocket climb and decides, then and there, to become an engineer is not wasting her time; she is doing exactly what our species has always done — reaching past the edge of the known because the reaching itself makes us larger.

6So perhaps the real choice is not whether to go, but how. Will we treat space as a new frontier to be fenced and sold, or as a shared inheritance to be governed with care? Will the view from out there teach us anything about the small blue world we are standing on?

7The stars are not going anywhere. The question is what kind of people we will be by the time we reach them.

6 · Writing

6a · Review (Scene → Detail → Verdict)
Lively, semi-formal register: hook, then what/where/when; one high point and one weak point with reasons; a recommendation naming the audience. A light future form + three evaluative adjectives. 150–200 words. Full model ("Walking on the Moon") in L9.
6b · Article (Position → Grant → Counter → Stand → Conclusion)
Grant then answer: hold one position throughout; grant the opponent's strongest point in earnest, then counter with a reason; Stand carries a prediction (future form) and a priority (modal). Required words: opportunity cost · prioritise · beneficial. 250–350 words. Full model ("Reaching for the Stars, Forgetting the Ground?") in L10.

9b · "Watch for" — misconceptions specific to this unit
Future forms (L7)Past tense after will ("will fell"); will where evidence calls for going to; stacking modals ("will might"). Drill: will + base for a belief, going to when evidence is present, one modal only.
Necessity modals (L7)to after a modal ("must to spend"); confusing mustn't (forbidden) with don't have to (not required). Drill: base form after the modal; mustn't ≠ don't have to.
Short answers & note completion (L3, L5)Over-writing. Part 1 and note completion want the fewest correct words; extra wrong detail can cancel a right answer, and over the word limit scores zero.
Fact vs opinion (L4)Treating a strongly-worded opinion as a fact. Require the signal word (argue, worry, should) as evidence it's a claim, not just a confident sentence.
Language & effect (L6)Naming a device with no effect, or praising a "nice word". Require the quotation/image AND its effect on the reader.
Review vs article (L9 vs L10–11)Review only describing (no verdict); article listing pros and cons or drifting to the granted side. Review = describe + judge + recommend; article = one held position, granted counter answered.
Listening match (L8)Word-match errors — picking a view because it shares a word with the extract. Drill: match the whole meaning; check the two distractors are said by nobody.
9c · Support / Stretch
Support — give the frames: "Admittedly, ___. However, ___." · "By 2050, space travel will ___." · "We must ___, and companies should ___." Word bank: wonder · opportunity cost · spin-off · beneficial · prioritise · feasible. Provide the review/article skeleton pre-labelled so students only fill content.
Stretch — chain a graded prediction and a priority in one sentence ("Costs will keep falling, so space could reach ordinary people — but governments must spend wisely"); grant the strongest objection, not the weakest; borrow one image from "The next horizon" into the article.

Voices on space — short extracts (Ex1)
Answers
1 D — A sense of wonder at how vast it is.  ·  2 A — It is less than one per cent of government spending.  ·  3 C — They both rely on satellite technology first built for space.  ·  4 B — Doubtful that it will happen soon.  ·  5 C — It was really about something deeper than the Moon itself.  ·  6 A — The programme paused for almost two years.  ·  7 B — Space research has produced useful medical technology.  ·  8 D — Build a Moon base first, then consider Mars.
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

[Extract 1] Honestly, I don't care about the science that much. For me it's just the feeling — looking up and knowing how much is out there that we'll never even see. That's why I keep coming back.

[Extract 2] People assume space programmes eat up huge slices of the budget, but in our country it's well under one per cent of government spending. Defence costs many times more.

[Extract 3] The satellite that gives you your weather app and the one that lets your phone find the nearest café — same basic technology, and it was first built for space.

[Extract 4] A holiday in orbit? Sure — if you've got a spare fortune. They keep promising it'll be for everyone 'soon', but I'll believe that when I see it.

[Extract 5] When I watched the old Moon-landing footage with my grandfather, he cried. I didn't understand it then. I think I do now — it wasn't about the Moon, really.

[Extract 6] After the launch failed, the whole programme stopped for nearly two years while engineers found out what went wrong.

[Extract 7] I get asked if space is a waste of money. My answer's always the same: look around your hospital. Half the monitoring kit in here traces back to keeping astronauts alive.

[Extract 8] The plan isn't Mars first. It's a permanent Moon base by the 2030s, and only then do we talk seriously about Mars.

A short interview (Ex2)
Answers
1 A — A drivable model of a Mars rover.  ·  2 C — Weekday mornings.  ·  3 A — Pay a small fee.  ·  4 B — A feeling of greater curiosity.  ·  5 A — Take it to three other cities.
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

PRESENTER: I'm here at the new 'Reaching Out' exhibition with its curator, Tom. Tom, what's the big draw? CURATOR: The centrepiece is a full-size model of a Mars rover that visitors can actually drive across a simulated surface. PRESENTER: And it's free for schools, I hear? CURATOR: Free for school groups on weekday mornings, yes. Families pay a small entry fee at weekends. PRESENTER: What do you most want visitors to take away? CURATOR: Not facts, really — a feeling. We want them to leave more curious than when they came in. PRESENTER: Any plans to take it on tour? CURATOR: We're hoping to move it to three other cities next year, if the funding comes through.
