1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped in four families, plus the academic chunks that lift your writing. Say them, then use them.

Move (keeping active)
energetic — full of energy and active
strengthen — to make or become stronger
flexibility — bending or adapting easily without breaking
posture — the way you hold your body, esp. when sitting
intense — very strong, extreme or forceful
discouraged — having lost confidence or the will to continue
automatic — done without conscious thought; happening by itself
Fuel (food & drink)
consume — to eat, drink or use up
consumption — the act of eating or using something
hydrate — to give the body the water it needs
dehydration — the harmful loss of water from the body
nutritious — containing what the body needs to stay healthy
nourish — to feed and care for so something grows healthy
processed — changed from its natural state in a factory
moderation — keeping within sensible limits; no extremes
alertness — being awake, attentive and quick to notice
misleading — giving a wrong idea or impression
Mind (stress & mood)
resilience — the ability to recover quickly from difficulty
burnout — extreme tiredness from too much stress over time
anxiety — a feeling of worry or unease
mindful — paying careful attention to the present moment
awareness — knowledge or perception of a situation
comparison — looking at things to see how they differ
supportive — giving help, encouragement or sympathy
overwhelm — to affect so strongly that you cannot cope
Rest (sleep & recovery)
recovery — a return to a normal or healthy state
restore — to bring back to a healthy or original state
refreshed — feeling revived and energetic, esp. after rest
drowsy — sleepy and not fully alert
consistent — regular and dependable over time
steady — firm, stable and not changing suddenly
relaxation — being free from tension and worry
sceptical — not easily convinced; having doubts
Academic chunks (lift your writing & speaking)
a balanced approach — a sensible method avoiding extremes
can lead to — links a cause to a likely result
in the long run — over a long period; eventually
rely on — to depend on something or someone
the very opposite of — completely different from
there is growing evidence that — research increasingly shows that
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several words. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

nourish → nourishing → nourishment
consume → consumption → consumer
hydrate → hydration → dehydration
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: wellbeing · burnout · resilience.

The reading texts
The Two-Minute Workout
Reading text

1For years, the advice was clear: to be healthy, you needed long sessions at the gym or a daily run. For busy students, that advice often led to nothing at all, because the task felt too big to begin. Now scientists are offering a gentler idea, and they have given it a memorable name: 'exercise snacks'.

2An exercise snack is a very short burst of activity, often lasting only one or two minutes, repeated several times across the day. It might be climbing the stairs quickly, doing ten squats while the kettle boils, or dancing to a single song. The movements are ordinary; what matters is that they are intense enough to raise the heart rate, even briefly.

3The benefits have surprised researchers. Studies suggest that several short bursts can improve heart fitness almost as effectively as one long workout. Because each snack is so brief, people are far more likely to actually do them, and far less likely to feel discouraged. There is a mental benefit too: a quick burst of movement between two hours of revision can sharpen concentration and lift a low mood.

4Fitting them into a school day is easier than it sounds. Experts suggest attaching a snack to something you already do — a habit they call 'stacking'. You might do star jumps after brushing your teeth, or a brisk walk around the building between lessons. The key is to choose a trigger you never skip, so the movement eventually becomes automatic.

5None of this replaces sport or play entirely; team games and longer activity still matter. But for the student who believes they 'have no time to exercise', the message is encouraging. Health, it turns out, can be built two minutes at a time.

The Energy Trap
Reading text

1Walk into almost any corner shop after school and you will see them: brightly coloured cans promising instant energy, sharper focus and better performance. Energy drinks have become one of the most popular products among teenagers, yet many young people have no idea what they are actually drinking.

2A single large can can contain as much caffeine as two cups of strong coffee, along with several spoonfuls of sugar. In small amounts, caffeine is harmless for most adults. The problem is the dose. Health experts recommend that teenagers consume no more than one hundred milligrams of caffeine a day; some energy drinks contain three times that in a single serving.

3The promised 'energy' is also misleading. The drinks do not create energy; they borrow it. Caffeine and sugar produce a short, sharp lift, usually followed an hour or two later by a crash that leaves the drinker more tired, and often hungrier, than before. Students who rely on them during exam season frequently report poorer sleep and difficulty concentrating — the very opposite of what they were hoping for.

4Some countries have responded with rules. In 2024, a number of supermarkets across the United Kingdom stopped selling the strongest energy drinks to anyone under sixteen, even before a law required it. Schools in several cities have removed them from vending machines entirely, replacing them with water stations and chilled fruit.

5There are simpler alternatives for a genuine lift. Drinking water is the cheapest: even mild dehydration can cause tiredness and headaches that are easily mistaken for hunger. A handful of nuts, a banana, or a short walk outside will all raise alertness without the later crash.

6None of this means the occasional can is dangerous. The advice from nutritionists is about awareness, not fear: read the label, know your limit, and remember that real, lasting energy comes from sleep, food and movement — not from a can.

Small Changes, Big Differences
Reading text

1A — Tom

2I used to drink two or three energy drinks a day, especially in the weeks before exams, convinced they kept me alert. The truth was the opposite: I was wired, then exhausted. I quit completely in January. The first few days were rough, and I still miss the taste sometimes, but my sleep is steadier and I am no longer spending half my allowance on cans. I had not realised how much they were costing me until I stopped.

3B — Lena

4I swapped the bus for my bike. It started as a way to avoid the crowded morning route, but it has become the best part of my day. I arrive at school properly awake instead of half asleep, and after a few months I noticed I could climb the stairs without losing my breath. I never once thought of it as 'training'. The only real drawback is the rain, which can turn a cheerful ride into a miserable one.

5C — Idris

6Before big swimming competitions I used to lie awake, replaying everything that could go wrong. A coach suggested writing for five minutes each night — just my worries, nothing neat. I was sceptical, and honestly I forgot to do it for the first fortnight. Once it became a habit, though, something shifted. Putting a fear into words somehow made it smaller and less frightening. I still get nervous, but the panic no longer controls me.

7D — Mei

8Joining the Saturday cooking club at our community centre was meant to be a one-off. Two years later I am still there. I learned to turn cheap ingredients into proper, balanced meals, which has genuinely changed how I eat. But what surprised me was how much the company mattered — laughing with the same group every week was as good for me as the food. These days I cook dinner for my whole family most evenings.

The Highlight Reel
Reading text

1Aisha Karim used to measure her days against a screen. Each evening she would scroll through photographs of classmates laughing on perfect beaches, winning competitions, and seemingly never having a bad hair day. By the time she put her phone down, her own life felt grey by comparison. 'I wasn't sad about anything real,' she remembers. 'I was sad that I wasn't someone else.'

2What Aisha did not yet understand is something psychologists have studied for years. Social media shows us other people's 'highlight reels' — the carefully chosen best moments — while we experience our own lives in full, including the boredom, the worry and the ordinary afternoons. Comparing the two is not just unfair; it is impossible to win.

3The turning point for Aisha was small. A teacher mentioned, almost in passing, that the feeds we scroll are 'edited, not lived'. The phrase stuck. Aisha began to notice how often a single post could shift her whole mood, and how rarely that shift had anything to do with her actual day.

4She did not delete her accounts — she is quick to say she is not against social media. Instead she ran an experiment. For one month she unfollowed any account that left her feeling worse, and started following ones that taught her something or made her laugh. She also set a rule: no scrolling for the first hour after waking, when, she had noticed, her mood was most easily knocked.

5The change was not dramatic, but it was real. 'I didn't suddenly love my life,' she says. 'I just stopped renting space in my head to strangers.' Her concentration improved, and so, slowly, did her sleep.

6Aisha now helps run a peer-support group at her school, where students talk openly about the pressure of comparison. She is careful not to preach. The point, she insists, is not that everyone should use their phone the way she does. It is simply that a feed is a choice, not a mirror — and that noticing how something makes you feel is the first, quietest step towards changing it.

6 · Speaking

Topic card · Being active in everyday life

We're going to talk about being active in everyday life. Take a moment to look at the points, then we'll discuss them together.

Talk for 1–2 minutes about:
  • how you stay active during a normal week (or wish you did)
  • a type of exercise or movement you enjoy, and one you dislike
  • whether you think you get enough movement in your day
  • the idea that short bursts of activity can be as useful as a long workout
Useful language
Open the long turn: “One thing I do to stay active is...”  /  “To be honest, I wish I...”
Develop (P-R-R-E): “...because [reason], which means [result]. For example,...”
Compare enjoy / dislike: “I really enjoy [x], whereas I can't stand [y] because...”
Give an opinion (discussion): “I'd argue that...”  /  “In the long run,...”
Respond to 'no time': “I understand why people say that, but even short bursts...”
Then discuss
  1. Should schools give students more time for physical activity during the day?
  2. Do you need expensive equipment or a gym to stay healthy?
  3. Some people say they 'have no time' to exercise. How would you respond to that?

7 · Grammar (Use of English)

5a · Cause & effect — join with a connector
  1. He skips breakfast ____ he loses focus by mid-morning.
  2. Mild dehydration ____ tiredness and headaches.
  3. She kept a steady bedtime. ____ she woke before her alarm.
5b · Advice — rewrite using the structure named
  1. "Drink more water." (It's worth -ing)
  2. "Don't rely on energy drinks." (You should…)
  3. "Put your phone in another room." (Why don't you…?)
5c · The -ing form (gerund)
  1. walk → ________ to school
  2. run → ________ every morning
  3. skip → ________ in the yard
5d · Comparatives
  1. Water is ________ (effective) than an energy drink for concentration.
  2. Walking is ________ (cheap) than a gym membership.
  3. The ____ (regular) your sleep, the ____ (good) you feel.

8 · Writing

6a · An informal letter — persuade a friend (120–160 words)
Shape: Open + your worry → Make it un-scary → Persuade + one small change now.   Stems: "I've been meaning to write since you said…" · "Before you say no — it's nothing intense…" · "Why don't we try it together?" · "Even before then, it's worth ___ — that way ___."
6b · A magazine article — staying healthy on a budget (120–160 words, semi-formal)
Shape: Title + name the myth → Low-cost ideas, each with a reason → Call to action.   Stems: "Many students are convinced that…" · "In reality this is misleading…" · "Water is far more effective than…, because…" · "In the long run, small habits beat any product — why not start today?"
