What Counts as a Sport?

Mark the four criteria

When officials decide whether an activity deserves to be called a sport, they rarely rely on instinct. Most governing bodies weigh four broad criteria: physical exertion, skill, organised competition, and a recognised governing body. An activity that satisfies all four has a strong claim; one that misses several does not.

Esports — competitive video gaming played for prizes in front of an audience — is often measured against exactly these tests, and the results are revealing.

Take organised competition. Here esports stands on firm ground. Professional titles run structured leagues, seasons, play-offs and world championships, with rules enforced by referees and anti-cheating systems as strict as any in traditional sport.

Governing bodies, too, increasingly exist. National esports federations have formed in dozens of countries, and international associations now represent the sport's interests, much as football or athletics organisations do.

Skill is rarely disputed. Top players train for hours every day to sharpen decision-making, teamwork and precision under pressure. Few who have watched a high-level match would call it easy.

The one criterion that remains contested is physical exertion. Critics argue that sitting at a screen, however intense the concentration, cannot compare to the bodily effort of running or swimming. Supporters reply that exertion comes in many forms — the rapid, repeated hand movements and split-second reactions of elite players are physical, even if they do not leave a player breathless.

This is the heart of the debate. If physical exertion is treated as the single decisive test, esports struggles. If it is one factor among four, esports scores well on the rest.

For now, the question stays open. Some bodies have embraced esports; others hold back. What is clear is that the four criteria, not personal taste, are how the argument is settled.
