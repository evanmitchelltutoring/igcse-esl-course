The next horizon

Circle the vivid images

Stand outside on a clear night and look up. That scatter of cold light has pulled at human beings for a hundred thousand years, and now, for the first time, the door to it is creaking open.

We are told this is the dawn of a new age. Rockets that once cost a fortune and burned up after a single flight now land themselves, upright, like dancers taking a bow. Companies speak of cities on Mars as casually as their grandparents spoke of crossing an ocean.

But step back from the advertising, and a harder question waits. Who, exactly, is this future for?

For now, a seat beyond the atmosphere costs more than most people will earn in a lifetime. The 'space for everyone' we are promised looks, on closer inspection, remarkably like space for a fortunate few — a glittering departure lounge with a velvet rope, and the rest of us pressed against the glass.

And yet it would be a thin, grey kind of caution that refused to dream at all. There is something in us that needs the horizon. The child who watches a rocket climb and decides, then and there, to become an engineer is not wasting her time; she is doing exactly what our species has always done — reaching past the edge of the known because the reaching itself makes us larger.

So perhaps the real choice is not whether to go, but how. Will we treat space as a new frontier to be fenced and sold, or as a shared inheritance to be governed with care? The stars are not going anywhere. The question is what kind of people we will be by the time we reach them.

Answer · mark your own

What each image does

1. The rope and glass picture a nightclub the reader is locked out of — it makes us feel excluded and resentful, so we side with fairness.
2. "makes us larger" turns dreaming into growth, not waste — it makes ambition sound noble and human, not childish.
3. Ending on character, not facts, leaves the reader with a moral choice — it lingers, and puts the decision on us.
