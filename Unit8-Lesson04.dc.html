Part 2: is it a fact or an opinion?

A balanced text mixes what can be checked with what people believe. Part 2 questions often test whether you can tell them apart.
A fact can be checked
A single mission can cost billions. — you could look it up. Facts give numbers, dates, events.
An opinion is a claim
Watch for signal words: argue, claim, believe, worry, should, it is not fair. These mark a view.

Counting the cost

Mark the three costs

Every rocket that climbs into the sky carries an invisible price tag. Space programmes are among the most expensive projects any government can undertake, and the question of whether that expenditure is justified has been argued for decades.

The most obvious cost is money. A single crewed mission can swallow billions, and the budget for a long-term project such as a Mars base would stretch across many years. Critics point to what economists call the opportunity cost: every sum allocated to a launch pad is a sum not spent on hospitals, schools or clean water. When public money is involved, they argue, the taxpayer has a right to ask whether such spending is wise — or whether it simply squanders resources that are urgently needed at home.

Supporters answer that the figures are smaller than they appear. In most countries, space agencies receive only a tiny fraction of national budgets, far less than is spent on defence. They also argue that the money is not fired into the sky and lost; it is paid to engineers, factories and universities on Earth.

Money, however, is not the only cost. Spaceflight is dangerous. Astronauts face hazards that most workers never imagine: extreme radiation, the failure of life-support systems, and months of isolation. The history of exploration is marked by tragic setbacks in which crews have lost their lives. Each such disaster forces agencies to pause, investigate and rebuild.

There is also a cost that is easy to overlook: the environment. Rocket launches release gases high into the atmosphere, and the growing number of satellites has filled orbit with debris that threatens future missions. As launches become more frequent, some scientists question whether the current pace is sustainable at all.
