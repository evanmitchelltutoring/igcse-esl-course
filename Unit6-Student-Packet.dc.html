1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped in four families. Say them, then use them in your writing and speaking.

Trends in Motion
trend — a general direction in which fashion is changing
fad — a style that is very popular briefly, then disappears
emerge — to start to appear or become known
influencer — a person who shapes others' choices by what they share online
mainstream — accepted by or popular with most ordinary people
revival — a return to popularity of something from the past
signature — clearly belonging to and identifying one person or brand
showcase — to show something in a way that draws attention to its best qualities
Dressing the Self
self-expression — showing who you are through what you make, wear, or do
individuality — the quality of being different from everyone else
confidence — the feeling of being sure of yourself
bold — strong, bright, and not afraid to stand out
understated — quietly stylish without being showy
versatile — able to be worn or used in many ways
tailored — made or adjusted to fit a particular person
wardrobe — all the clothes a person owns
The Cost Underneath
fast fashion — cheap clothes produced very quickly to follow trends
ethical — morally right; fair to the people and planet involved
exploitation — treating people unfairly to benefit from their work
disposable — meant to be thrown away after little use
garment — a single piece of clothing
mass-produce — to make large amounts cheaply by machine
second-hand — already owned or used by someone else
thrift — to buy used clothes cheaply from second-hand shops
Fitting In or Standing Out
conform — to behave or look the way most others do
norm — what is usual or expected in a group
peer pressure — the push to act like the people your own age
stand out — to be easily noticed as different
blend in — to look the same as everyone around you
stereotype — a fixed, oversimplified idea about a type of person
acceptance — being welcomed as part of a group
uniform — special clothes for a school or job
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

express → expression → expressive
conform → conformity → conformist
produce → production → productive
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: self-expression · exploitation · conform.

The reading texts
How a Trend Travels
Reading text

1Five years ago, almost no one at my school wore wide-legged jeans. Now, on any morning, half the corridor is in them. I started to wonder how a single look spreads so fast — and why it fades just as quickly.

2A trend usually begins small. A designer shows something unusual on a runway, or a teenager in one city films a thirty-second clip of an outfit. Most of these ideas go nowhere. But every so often one of them is picked up by an influencer with millions of followers, and within days it is everywhere. Last spring, a student in Seoul posted a video of herself styling an oversized blazer with shorts. The clip went viral. Three weeks later, shops in Bangkok were selling almost the same blazer, and my classmates were wearing it without knowing where it came from.

3This is the strange thing about modern fashion: a look can travel from one bedroom to a hundred countries before anyone can explain why it matters. Social media has made the journey faster than ever. In the past, a style might take a year to move from a fashion capital to an ordinary high street. Today it can happen in a fortnight.

4Speed has a cost, though. The faster a trend rises, the faster it falls. By the time a look becomes truly mainstream — sold cheaply in every shop, worn by everyone — the people who started it have usually moved on. What felt fresh in March can feel tired by September. Some styles return years later as a revival, dressed up as something new, and the cycle begins again.

5So the next time you notice everyone suddenly wearing the same thing, it is worth asking two questions. Where did this come from? And how long before it disappears? The answers say a lot — not only about clothes, but about how quickly we copy one another.

Why I Stopped Dressing for Other People
Reading text

1For most of secondary school, I dressed to disappear. I chose whatever the others were wearing — the same shoes, the same bag, the same neutral colours — because being noticed felt dangerous. If no one looked at me, no one could judge me. It worked, in a way. I blended in completely. I also felt completely invisible.

2The change began with a coat. I found it in a second-hand shop: deep green, slightly too big, with large gold buttons. It was nothing like what anyone at school wore. For two weeks it hung in my wardrobe while I argued with myself. Wearing it would mean standing out, and standing out meant questions.

3I wore it on a Monday. The first comment came before I reached my classroom — but it was not the comment I feared. I love that, a girl I barely knew said. It is so you. I had no idea what so me meant; I had spent years making sure I had no recognisable style at all. Yet the phrase stayed with me. For the first time, my clothes had said something, and someone had listened.

4After that, I began to experiment. Not dramatically — I am not a bold dresser even now. But I started choosing things because I liked them, not because they were safe. A scarf in a colour that made me happy. Shoes that were comfortable rather than fashionable. Slowly, my wardrobe began to feel less like a uniform and more like a reflection of me.

5What surprised me most was the effect on my confidence. I had always assumed confidence came first, and that confident people then dressed boldly. For me it was the other way around. Choosing clothes that felt true made me stand a little straighter. The green coat did not make me a different person. It simply let the person I already was become visible.

6I am not saying everyone should dress to be noticed. Some people feel most themselves in plain, quiet clothes, and that is its own kind of honesty. The point is not loud or quiet, bold or modest. The point is choice — wearing what reflects you, rather than what hides you. Looking back, the saddest part of those early years was not that I dressed badly. It was that I dressed like someone trying very hard not to exist.

7These days, when a younger student asks me for advice about clothes, I give the same short answer every time: wear the coat.

The Real Price of Cheap Clothes
Reading text

1A T-shirt can now cost less than a cup of coffee. To a shopper, this looks like good news. To the planet, and to the people who make our clothes, it is a warning sign.

2The system behind these low prices is called fast fashion. Brands copy the latest styles, manufacture them at enormous speed, and sell them cheaply, knowing buyers will return within weeks for the next look. To keep prices down, much of this clothing is mass-produced in factories where workers are paid very little. Reports have repeatedly described long hours, unsafe buildings, and wages too low to live on. The cheapness we enjoy in the shop is partly paid for by someone else, somewhere we never see.

3The environmental cost is just as serious. Making clothes uses huge amounts of water and energy, and the dyes often pollute rivers. Worse, fast fashion is designed to be temporary. Garments are made to fall out of style — and sometimes to fall apart — within a season, so we keep buying. The result is mountains of waste. A great deal of discarded clothing is burned or buried, and synthetic fabrics can take centuries to break down.

4Defenders of fast fashion make two arguments. First, it makes style affordable: a teenager on a small budget can dress well without spending a fortune. Second, the industry provides jobs in countries that need them. Both points are true, and they should not be dismissed. Cheap clothing is not simply evil, and the workers who depend on these jobs would suffer if every factory closed overnight.

5But affordability and jobs do not require a system built on waste and low pay. The problem is not that clothes are cheap; it is that the true costs are hidden and pushed onto others. There are better paths. Buying fewer, better-made items reduces waste. Choosing second-hand keeps clothing in use for longer. Supporting brands that pay fairly and produce less rewards the right behaviour. None of these alone will fix the industry, but together they shift the pressure.

6In the end, the question is not whether we can afford fast fashion. Clearly we can — the prices are tiny. The real question is whether we can afford its true cost: the rivers, the waste, and the people behind the seams. Once you have seen that price, the cheap T-shirt never looks quite so cheap again.

The Grey Army
Reading text

1Every morning, two thousand of us pour through the gates in the same grey. From the bridge above, we are not students; we are a tide, a single grey animal breathing in and out at the bell. Our uniform is meant to make us equal. Sometimes it just makes us disappear.

2I have worn this grey for five years, and I have felt both things it does. On my worst mornings, when my shoes are scuffed and my hair will not sit, the uniform is a kindness — a curtain I can hide behind, where no one measures me against anyone else. We are all the same grey, and the same grey cannot be mocked.

3But there are other mornings. Mornings when I want to be a colour, not a crowd. When the grey feels less like a curtain and more like a lid, pressing two thousand restless lives into one flat shape. On those mornings I notice the rebellions, tiny and everywhere: a bright lace, a folded sleeve, a badge worn just out of a teacher's sightline. Small flames, flickering against the grey.

4That is the truth no rule can erase. You can dress us the same, but you cannot make us the same. The need to be ourselves does not vanish; it only shrinks, hides, waits. Press it down here, and it pops up there — in a drawing inside a shoe, a song hummed in a corridor, a way of walking that is unmistakably yours.

5So dress us in grey, if you must. We will wear it. But look closely at the tide as it passes, and you will see it everywhere: the colour leaking through. We were never one grey animal. We were two thousand, each of us burning quietly to be seen.

    7 · Grammar (Use of English)

    5a · Present continuous for trends (is/are + -ing · more and more)

    Use is/are + verb-ing for what's happening or changing now. Add more and more for a growing trend.

    1. Fix: "More people is wearing second-hand clothes."
    2. Fix: "The style is change quickly this year."
    3. Make a trend: (young people / follow / influencers)
    4. Rewrite with more and more: "Bright colours are popular now, and every year more so."
    5b · Reported speech (say / tell · backshift)
    1. Fix: "She said me that she liked my scarf."
    2. Report: "I will wear black tomorrow," he said.
    3. Report: "I bought this yesterday," she told me.
    4. Report the question: "Do you like my hat?" he asked.

    8 · Writing — your Style Profile

    6a · The Style Interview — a magazine write-up (120–200 words)
    Shape: Introduce → Report → Round off.   Report, don't transcript: retell their words in your voice (she told me… he explained…) with backshift — not a Q-and-A. Set the person up first; end on what their answers reveal. Use 4+ target words (self-expression · identity · confidence · signature · bold · modest).
    6b · Describe Someone's Style — a descriptive piece (120–160 words)
    Shape: Scene → Detail → Verdict.   Show, don't tell: concrete detail and expanded noun phrases (opinion before fact — "a battered old leather jacket"); hold the present continuous; end on what the style reveals. Use 4+ target words (self-expression · distinctive · bold · understated · signature · identity).
