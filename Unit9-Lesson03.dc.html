Teaching myself, one tab at a time

Mark what made her succeed

When Maya decided she wanted to make her own animated short films, she did not sign up for a class. There were no animation courses at her school, and the nearest studio was a two-hour bus ride away. Instead, she opened her laptop, typed 'how to animate for beginners' into a search bar, and pressed enter.

That single search led her to thousands of free tutorials. Some were excellent; many were not. At first the sheer number of choices was overwhelming. Maya quickly learned that being a self-directed learner meant more than just watching videos — it meant deciding which videos were worth her time. She became resourceful, testing a technique for ten minutes and abandoning it the moment it led nowhere.

Progress was slow. Her first animation lasted four seconds and took a week to make. But every small success gave her the motivation to continue. She kept a simple notebook in which she wrote down what had gone wrong and what she would try next. Looking back, she says this habit of reflection mattered more than any single tutorial.

What surprised Maya most was how much her curiosity grew the more she learned. A question about movement led her to study how real animals walk; a question about colour led her to read about light. One skill opened a door to another. Within a year she had acquired a level of proficiency that astonished her teachers — none of whom had taught her a thing.

Of course, learning alone has its dangers. Without a teacher to correct them, self-taught learners can practise their mistakes until those mistakes start to feel normal. Maya admits she spent months animating in a way she later had to unlearn. Teaching yourself, she says, is freedom — but freedom with no one to catch your errors.
