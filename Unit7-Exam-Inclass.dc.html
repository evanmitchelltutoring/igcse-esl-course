Insert — the text

Read the whole text once before you begin. Paragraphs are numbered.

1  For most of the last century, sport climbing was not a sport at all — at least, not in the eyes of the people who decide such things. It was a pastime for a few brave enthusiasts on distant cliffs, admired but never counted. Critics said it was too dangerous, too individual, and impossible to judge fairly. The idea that climbers might one day win Olympic medals would have seemed absurd.

2  The first thing that changed was the setting. Climbers moved indoors, onto artificial walls where every hold could be measured and every route made identical for each competitor. What had been a wild, unrepeatable adventure became a fair, repeatable contest. Suddenly the old objection — that climbing could not be judged fairly — began to fall away.

3  Next came organisation. Climbers formed a single international federation, which standardised the rules, trained the judges, and ran a proper calendar of world events. This mattered more than it might sound. A recognised governing body is one of the criteria officials look for, and by building one, climbing quietly met a test it had always failed before.

4  Then the audiences arrived. Televised events drew crowds that surprised even the organisers; a young, global following filled arenas and streams. Sponsors, sensing momentum, followed the audience. The sport that "nobody watched" was now, inconveniently for its critics, watched by millions — and that visibility gave it a credibility no argument had managed to.

5  Finally, in 2021, climbers stood on an Olympic podium. The activity once waved away as a daredevil hobby had passed every test the doubters once set it. It had not changed what it was, in essence — people still climbed — but it had changed everything around itself: the setting, the structure, the audience. And this, the writer suggests, is the real lesson for the esports debate: the line for what "counts" is not fixed; it moves when an activity organises itself, proves it can be judged fairly, and wins an audience.
