The most useful thing you'll get wrong

Mark the three steps

Most people treat a mistake as something to feel ashamed of and forget as quickly as possible. Yet the people who improve fastest do almost the opposite: they slow down, study the mistake, and squeeze every lesson out of it. For them, an error is not a verdict on their ability. It is information.

Consider how a child learns to walk. They fall hundreds of times, but no one calls a falling baby a failure. Each stumble quietly adjusts their balance for the next attempt. Somewhere on the road to adulthood, many of us lose this attitude. The problem is that a mistake you hide is a mistake you cannot learn from.

The first step, then, is simply to look. When you get something wrong, resist the urge to move on. Instead, analyse it: ask exactly where your thinking went astray and why. A student who only notices that an answer was wrong learns very little; a student who works out the reason rarely repeats the error.

The second step is to invite other eyes. We are often blind to our own habits, and honest, constructive feedback can reveal a weakness we have walked past a hundred times. Feedback can sting, but the discomfort fades far faster than the cost of repeating the same mistake for years.

The third step is reflection. After feedback, take a quiet moment to ask what you will do differently. This is not vague worrying; it is a deliberate plan. Many successful learners keep a record of their errors precisely so that patterns become visible over time.

None of this is comfortable, which is why determination matters so much. Studying your own mistakes means accepting, again and again, that you are not yet as good as you would like to be. The reward is that each setback becomes a step rather than a wall.
