4 · Reading tasks

4a · "A Picture in Ten Seconds" — short answers (Part 1)
Answer key · 4a1 about ten seconds.   2 a dataset.   3 it uses the patterns it has learned to generate something new that fits the words.   4 because a seemingly original picture might be built from the work of thousands of artists who were never asked.   5 a passing novelty — fun for now, not real art. Mark for precision; over-long answers with wrong extra detail can cancel.
4b · "The Thing a Machine Cannot Feel" — language & effect (Part 2)
Answer key · 4b1 the "twenty years" stresses the long, costly human effort behind real skill; repeating it weighs that effort against the machine's ten seconds.   2 that the machine has no memories, no life, no relationships to draw on — no feeling behind the work.   3 accept e.g. "I will not pretend the camera did not terrify painters once" / "New tools have always frightened…".   4 because the pictures "come from nowhere" — made by something that wanted nothing, remembered nothing, risked nothing. Full credit needs the device/word AND its effect.
4c · "Where Does an AI Learn to Draw?" — note completion (Part 3)
Answer key · 4cCollected by software that will scrape them (from the internet) · it learns patterns (not single pictures) · three fairness words: attribution · consent · compensation · companies' defence: it learns like a (human) student studying the masters. Accept 1–3 words / short paraphrase.
4d · "Should a Machine Win an Art Prize?" — summary (Part 4)
Answer key · 4dSupporters (any 2): judge on artistic merit alone; humans are still involved (prompting, selecting, editing); it is simply the newest medium, as photography once was.   Opponents (any 2): unfair — trained on human work that went unpaid; it undermines art's value and artists' income; it damages the prize's credibility.   Middle ground: not a ban but transparency / labelling (some competitions add a separate AI category).   Overall: a cautious view — accept the work but label it honestly; the deeper questions of consent and payment are not settled. Reward own-words summary; penalise lifted sentences and over-length.

6 · Writing

6a · Speech "Should our art prize accept AI entries?" (Hook+stance → Grant → Counter → Stand → Call to action)
Stems: "Ask yourself this…" · "There is no denying that…" · "But in reality…" · "So when you vote…" Concession linker + 3 target words; address the audience directly.
6b · Argumentative essay "A machine can never truly make art." (Stance → Concession → Reasoning → Own argument → Conclusion)
Grade certainty: could / might → must / can't. Concession linkers + 5 target words; resolve the stance, don't merely repeat it.

9b · "Watch for" — misconceptions specific to this unit
Modals of speculation (L7)Using must for a mere guess, or a wrong past form ("must went / must to be"). Drill the certainty ladder (must = sure yes, can't = sure no, might/could = maybe) and must/might/can't + have + participle for the past.
Concession linkers (L8)Doubling ("Although… but…") and comma splices with however. One concession word per clause; however takes a semicolon or full stop, not a comma.
Summary (L6)Copying sentences wholesale and running over length. Require own words, points only (no examples/repetition), and strict word count.
Language & effect (L4)Naming a device with no effect ("it's a short sentence"). Require the effect on the reader AND the word/technique that creates it.
Speech vs essay (L10 vs L11–12)A speech that reads like an essay (no audience) or an essay that turns chatty. Speech = direct address + call to action; essay = formal, balanced, resolves the stance.
Debate (L11–12 prep)Arguing both sides of an assigned motion. Commit to one side; concede a real opposing point only to rebut it.
9c · Support / Stretch
Support — give the frames: "It must have ___ because ___." · "Although ___, ___." · "There is no denying that ___, but ___." Word bank: generate · dataset · imitate · consent · authentic · merit · legitimate. Provide the speech/essay skeleton pre-labelled so students only fill content.
Stretch — chain a concession and a graded-certainty claim in one sentence ("While the image is striking, it could only be recombining what it has seen"); rebut the strongest opposing point, not the weakest; deliver the speech from notes, not a script.

EAL note: reward communication that succeeds despite minor slips; do not over-penalise L1-influenced error where meaning is clear. Speech 150–200 words; essay 120–160 words.
