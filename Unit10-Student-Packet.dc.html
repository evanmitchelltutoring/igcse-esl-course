1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped in four strands. Say them, then use them in your writing and speaking.

A changing climate
degradation — damage to something, especially the environment
emissions — gases released into the air, especially from burning fuels
mitigate — to make something less harmful or serious
offset — to balance one thing against another to reduce its effect
scarcity — a situation in which there is not enough of something
A fairer world
deprivation — the lack of basic things people need for a decent life
discrimination — unfair treatment based on a person's group
disparity — a clear, often unfair difference
equitable — fair and reasonable for everyone's situation
inclusion — making sure everyone is treated as part of a group
marginalised — pushed to the edges of society; treated as unimportant
privilege — a special advantage only some people have
vulnerable — easily harmed or hurt
People on the move
asylum — protection given to someone who has fled danger
diaspora — people of one origin living spread across the world
heritage — traditions and culture passed down within a group
host community — the community that receives and welcomes newcomers
integration — becoming a full part of a community
refugee — a person forced to leave their country to escape danger
relocate — to move to a new place to live or work
resettle — to help people start a new life in a different place
Working together
accountability — the duty to take responsibility for your actions
coalition — a group formed by parties or countries to act together
commitment — a firm promise to do something
compromise — an agreement where each side gives up part
mutual — shared or felt by each side towards the other
negotiate — to discuss in order to reach an agreement
solidarity — unity and support among people who share aims
treaty — a formal written agreement between countries
Argument chunks — for the essay & debate
it's part of a bigger picture — link a small thing to a large one
it's not enough on its own — concede a limit
arguably — hedge; not certain
on balance — signal a weighed-up view
you're arguably right that…, although… — concede, then answer
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

equity → equitable → equitably
integrate → integration → integrated
negotiate → negotiation → negotiable
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: emissions · disparity · solidarity.

The reading texts
Small Choices, Big Picture
Reading text

1A few years ago, switching off a light or refusing a plastic bag felt almost pointless to many teenagers. What difference could one person make against a problem as vast as a changing climate? Today, that mood is shifting. Across the world, young people are deciding that small choices are not the whole answer, but they are not nothing either.

2Take Maya, a sixteen-year-old who began by cutting the meat in her family's meals twice a week. On its own, her choice changes very little. Yet when she persuaded her school canteen to offer the same option, hundreds of students were suddenly eating differently. Her single decision had multiplied. This is the pattern that researchers keep noticing: individual actions matter most when they spread.

3Of course, not everyone agrees on where the focus should lie. Some argue that asking teenagers to recycle or take shorter showers shifts attention away from the real source of emissions — large industries and the choices of governments. They have a point. No amount of careful shopping by one student will match the impact of a national energy policy. But others reply that habits and policies are linked: people who change their own behaviour are also the people most likely to vote, campaign and push leaders to act.

4Perhaps the most useful way to see it is this. Individual choices and big systems are not rivals; they feed each other. A teenager who refuses fast fashion today may run a company tomorrow. The student who organises a clean-up may one day write the law. Small choices, in other words, are often where bigger change quietly begins.

5None of this means the task is easy, and pretending the problem is solved would help no one. But the gloom that once surrounded the subject is giving way to something more practical: a sense that ordinary people, and especially young ones, have more influence than they were once told.

The Homework Gap
Reading text

1Every evening, two students sit down to do the same homework. One opens a laptop in a quiet room and connects to fast, reliable internet. The other shares a single phone with younger siblings, hunts for a signal near the window, and waits for pages to load. They are set the same task and judged by the same standard. They do not start from the same place.

2Teachers have a name for this: the homework gap. It describes the difference in learning that comes not from ability or effort, but from what students can access at home. A recent school survey found that nearly one in three students did not have a quiet place to study, and roughly one in five had no dependable internet connection. For these students, a piece of online homework is not simply harder; it is sometimes impossible to finish on time.

3The gap is easy to miss because it is invisible in the classroom. A student who hands in unfinished work may look careless, when in truth they ran out of data at nine o'clock the night before. Marks fall, confidence follows, and a problem that began with resources slowly turns into a belief that the student is 'just not good at school'.

4Some schools have started to respond. A few now lend laptops and portable internet devices to students who need them. Others keep the library open late so that anyone can find a quiet desk and a steady connection. One head teacher described the change simply: when the tools were shared more equally, the marks of the lowest-achieving group rose within a single term.

5None of these measures removes inequality entirely. A borrowed laptop does not buy a quiet home. But they show something important — that part of what looks like a gap in talent is really a gap in access, and that access is something schools and communities can choose to change.

Starting Over
Reading text

1When a family arrives in a new country, the first weeks can feel overwhelming. The language is unfamiliar, the streets are confusing, and simple tasks — registering at a school, opening an account, finding a doctor — suddenly require help. How quickly newcomers settle often depends less on luck than on the welcome they receive.

2Many communities have found practical ways to help. Free language classes are among the most valuable, because without the local language almost everything else is harder. Some towns run 'welcome groups', where volunteers meet new arrivals, explain how local services work, and introduce them to others. Schools play a quiet but powerful role too: when children make friends quickly, their parents often follow them into the community. A few places have set up job-matching schemes that connect newcomers' existing skills with local employers, so that a trained nurse or engineer is not left delivering leaflets for years.

3The difficulties, however, are real. Many newcomers struggle to have their qualifications recognised, and find themselves starting careers again from the beginning. Others face loneliness, especially if they are far from family and unsure of the customs around them. Prejudice, though not universal, can make daily life tiring in ways that are hard to explain to those who have never experienced it. And paperwork — endless forms in an unfamiliar language — can turn a small task into a day-long ordeal.

4What seems to help most is patience on both sides. Newcomers who are given time and support tend to contribute a great deal: starting businesses, filling shortages, and enriching the places they join. The towns that welcome them well rarely regret it.

We're All Downstream
Reading text

1Imagine a single river running through many villages. The village at the top can pour whatever it likes into the water — and it is the villages below that drink it. This, in a sentence, is the problem of global responsibility. We are all downstream of someone, and upstream of someone else.

2For years we have argued about who should clean the river. Wealthier nations, the argument goes, built their comfort by burning fuel and clearing land long before anyone counted the cost; surely they owe the most to fix it? It is a powerful case, and an honest one. Those with the broadest shoulders can carry the heaviest load.

3But honesty cuts both ways. Pointing only at the rich is comfortable, yet it is not the whole truth. A problem this large cannot be solved by a handful of countries writing cheques while everyone else waits. Rivers do not respect borders, and neither do solutions. The village downstream still has to decide what it tips into the water.

4So here is the harder, fairer idea: shared responsibility, measured by capacity. Those who can do more, should. Those who can do less, still do something. Not blame, not guilt — contribution. It is less satisfying than a villain, and far more useful.

5Will it be easy? Of course not. Cooperation is slow, frustrating, and full of broken promises. Treaties are signed and quietly ignored. And yet, again and again, it is the only thing that has ever worked. We cleaned the hole in the ozone layer not by shouting across the river, but by agreeing, together, to stop.

6The water keeps moving. The question is not whether we are connected — we plainly are — but whether we are willing to act as though we are. We are all downstream. We had better start behaving like it.

5 · Grammar (Use of English)

5a · Hedging (be cautious)

Soften absolute claims with may · might · tend to · arguably · in many cases. One hedge is enough.

  1. Soften: "Recycling solves the climate crisis."
  2. Fix: "It may possibly arguably help."
  3. Soften: "Everyone always wastes food."
5b · Subordination (join & weigh)

Join and weigh ideas with although · while · because · even though. A subordinate clause needs a main clause.

  1. Join: "Although it is difficult. We must try."
  2. Join with while: "Rich nations owe most. Poorer ones should act too."

6 · Writing

6a · A magazine article — 'A Changing World' feature (150–200 words)
Shape: Headline → Hook → why it matters to readers your age → one or two real actions → an imperative call to action.   Lively, encouraging register (never preachy); target words (sustainable · consumption · accountability · collaborate · commitment · empowerment).
6b · An argumentative essay — "Who should do more?" (120–160 words)
Shape (S-E-R): Situation (set neutrally) → Evaluation (weigh both sides, with hedging) → Recommendation (measured, subordinated).   Even-handed; hedging + subordination; target words (accountability · capacity · equitable · mutual · disparity). Weigh, then decide — don't pick one side and defend it.
