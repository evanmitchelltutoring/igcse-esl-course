Why do we look up?

Mark each reason we reach

For as long as people have existed, they have gazed at the night sky and wondered what lies beyond it. Long before rockets or telescopes, farmers used the stars to mark the seasons and sailors used them to find their way home. Yet our interest in space has never been purely practical. Something about the darkness overhead has always stirred a deep sense of wonder — a feeling that there is more to discover than we can ever see from the ground.

That curiosity eventually carried human beings off the planet altogether. The first Moon landing in 1969 was a milestone that millions watched on television, holding their breath as a person stepped onto another world for the first time. For many who saw it, the moment was less about science than about inspiration: if humans could reach the Moon, what else might they achieve? Teachers reported that children who had never thought about engineering suddenly wanted to build spacecraft.

Of course, curiosity is not the only reason nations spend enormous sums on space. During the twentieth century, sending astronauts beyond Earth became a way of demonstrating power. A successful launch brought a country prestige, suggesting to the world that its scientists and engineers were among the best. Some historians argue that the race to the Moon was driven as much by competition between rival nations as by any genuine wish to expand human knowledge.

Today the picture is more complicated. Governments still run space programmes, but private companies now design and launch their own rockets, sometimes carrying paying passengers. Critics complain that this turns a noble adventure into a business, and that the wonder of the early years has been replaced by advertising. Supporters reply that competition has made spaceflight cheaper — and that lower costs will eventually allow far more people to reach for the stars.
