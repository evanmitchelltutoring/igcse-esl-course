Insert — the text

Read the whole text once before you begin. Paragraphs are numbered.

1  On a grey December morning, the supply boat reached the lighthouse on Carrick Rock two weeks late, delayed by storms. The three keepers who lived there should have been waiting on the jetty. Instead, the rock was silent.

2  The boat's captain, a careful man named Hale, climbed the long steps alone. He found the great door closed but unlocked. Inside, everything was strangely ordinary. The lamps had been cleaned. A meal sat half-eaten on the table, the bread now hard as stone. Two of the three oilskin coats were missing from their hooks by the door; the third still hung in its place. And the clock on the wall had stopped at 9.04.

3  The keepers were never seen again. When investigators arrived, they searched the logbook for an answer. The early entries were calm and routine. Then, three days before the boat was due, the handwriting changed. One keeper had written of a great storm and of seas washing over the gallery, although records from passing ships showed that the weather those nights had been mild and clear. The final entry read only: Storm ended. All quiet now.

4  Theories came quickly, and none of them held. Some said a freak wave had swept all three men from the rocks, but only two coats were gone, and no keeper would climb down to a wild sea without his oilskin. Others suggested the men had quarrelled; yet there was no boat, no blood, and no way off the rock but the sea. A few whispered of madness brought on by the endless wind.

5  What troubled investigators most was the logbook. If the weather had truly been calm, why had the keeper written of a storm that never came? Had the men seen something the passing ships could not? Or had one of them, alone in the dark, begun to imagine the sea rising around a tower that stood, in truth, perfectly still? A century later, no one has ever explained what happened on Carrick Rock, and perhaps no one ever will.

Marking — how your answers are judged
BandWhat it looks like
StrongAll factual answers correct and precise; summary in your own words, points only, within the word count. For method questions, you identify the writer's choice and support it with the exact words that carry it, explaining their effect on the reader.
SecureMost factual answers correct; summary covers the main points with some own wording. You show the writer builds a mood and point to some evidence, though the explanation of how the words work may be brief.
EAL note: You are marked on understanding, not perfect English. A correct idea in simple, clear words earns full marks. Use the class stems — "The writer makes it feel eerie because the word ___ suggests…" — to show your thinking.

D1 summary (6): reward, in own words — Hale found the lighthouse empty but ordinary (clean lamps, a half-eaten meal, a stopped clock, two coats gone). Theories failed: a freak wave can't explain the remaining coat; a quarrel leaves no boat, blood or exit; and the logbook records a storm that ships say never came. Up to 4 for content, up to 2 for own-words expression within 50–70 words. Penalise lifted sentences and over-length.
E1 (2) "Strangely ordinary" is unsettling because the calm, everyday scene is exactly what makes the absence of the men so wrong — we expect drama and find a cleaned lamp and a half-eaten meal, so the horror is in the normality.
E2 (2) The unanswered questions leave the reader suspended in doubt — the writer refuses a solution, so the mystery stays open and the eerie feeling lingers.
E3 (4) No — the writer signals it may never be solved. Reward two supported points, e.g. "Theories came quickly, and none of them held" and "no one has ever explained what happened … and perhaps no one ever will." Full marks require the words quoted, not just the idea.
