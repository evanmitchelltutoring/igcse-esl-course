Why I Stopped Dressing for Other People

Read · then read again

For most of secondary school, I dressed to disappear. I chose whatever the others were wearing — the same shoes, the same bag, the same neutral colours — because being noticed felt dangerous. If no one looked at me, no one could judge me. It worked, in a way. I blended in completely. I also felt completely invisible.

The change began with a coat. I found it in a second-hand shop: deep green, slightly too big, with large gold buttons. It was nothing like what anyone at school wore. For two weeks it hung in my wardrobe while I argued with myself. Wearing it would mean standing out, and standing out meant questions.

I wore it on a Monday. The first comment came before I reached my classroom — but it was not the comment I feared. I love that, a girl I barely knew said. It is so you. I had no idea what so me meant; I had spent years making sure I had no recognisable style at all. Yet the phrase stayed with me. For the first time, my clothes had said something, and someone had listened.

After that, I began to experiment. Not dramatically — I am not a bold dresser even now. But I started choosing things because I liked them, not because they were safe. A scarf in a colour that made me happy. Shoes that were comfortable rather than fashionable. Slowly, my wardrobe began to feel less like a uniform and more like a reflection of me.

What surprised me most was the effect on my confidence. I had always assumed confidence came first, and that confident people then dressed boldly. For me it was the other way around. Choosing clothes that felt true made me stand a little straighter. The green coat did not make me a different person. It simply let the person I already was become visible.

I am not saying everyone should dress to be noticed. Some people feel most themselves in plain, quiet clothes, and that is its own kind of honesty. The point is not loud or quiet, bold or modest. The point is choice — wearing what reflects you, rather than what hides you.

1. Being noticed felt dangerous — "if no one looked at me, no one could judge me."
2. Invisible — the word that ends paragraph 1 ("completely invisible").
3. For the first time her clothes "said something, and someone listened" — after years of having no style.

5. Not loud or quiet, bold or modest — the point is choice: wearing what reflects you, not what hides you.
