1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped in four families. Say them, then use them in your writing and speaking.

Plants & Landscapes
adapt — to change in order to survive better
barren — too poor to grow plants; bare and lifeless
drought — a long period with little or no rain
ecosystem — living things + the environment they depend on
lush — growing thickly and healthily; rich and green
thrive — to grow strongly and do very well
Animal Migration
migration — the seasonal movement of animals between regions
instinct — a natural behaviour you don't have to learn
navigate — to find the way across difficult land or water
predator — an animal that hunts and eats other animals
flock — a large group of birds moving together
vast — extremely large in size, amount or extent
City Environments
urban — relating to a town or city (opposite: rural)
sustainable — able to continue without damaging the environment
renewable — able to be replaced naturally, such as sun or wind
emission — a harmful gas or substance sent into the air
congestion — overcrowding, especially with traffic
infrastructure — the basic systems a place needs (roads, water)
Conservation
conservation — the protection of nature and its resources
endangered — at serious risk of dying out completely
restoration — returning something to its healthy condition
preserve — to keep something safe for the future
habitat — the natural home of an animal or plant
species — a group of living things that can breed together
neglect — to fail to care for something properly
fragile — easily damaged, broken or harmed
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several words. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

sustain → sustainable → sustainability
conserve → conservation → conservationist
migrate → migration → migratory
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: habitat · ecosystem · conservation.

The reading texts
The Plants That Break the Rules
Reading text

1We tend to think of plants as quiet, patient things that stay where they are put. A few species, however, seem determined to break every rule we expect them to follow.

2Take the welwitschia, which grows in the Namib Desert of southern Africa. It produces only two leaves in its entire life, yet it can live for more than a thousand years. The leaves never fall; instead they grow steadily, split and fray at the tips until the plant looks like a tangled heap of old ribbon. Botanists call it a living fossil, because it has barely changed in millions of years.

3Far to the east, in the rainforests of Sumatra, the titan arum takes a different approach to survival. Once every few years it unfurls a flower taller than an adult, which gives off a powerful smell of rotting meat. Visitors often hold their noses, but the stink is no accident: it draws in the flies and beetles that the plant depends on to carry its pollen. The bloom lasts barely two days before it collapses.

4The resurrection plant is stranger still. During a drought it curls into a dry, brown ball that looks completely dead and can be blown across the ground by the wind. Yet add a little water and, within hours, it unrolls and turns green again, as though nothing had happened. Some specimens have been brought back to life after decades on a shelf.

5Perhaps the boldest of all is the dancing plant of South Asia, whose small side-leaves twitch and rotate in response to warmth, light and even music. Nobody is quite sure why it moves; one theory is that the motion helps the plant find the brightest patches of sun.

6What these plants share is a refusal to be ordinary. Each has found an unusual answer to the same question every living thing must face: how to survive.

Four Great Journeys
Reading text

1A The Arctic tern

2No animal travels further. Each year the Arctic tern flies from the Arctic, where it breeds, all the way to the seas around Antarctica and back — a round trip of around 70,000 kilometres. Because it follows the summer at both ends of the planet, it sees more daylight than any other creature on Earth. It rarely lands, snatching fish from the surface of the sea as it goes, and a single tern may cover three million kilometres in its lifetime.

3B The monarch butterfly

4The monarch's journey is remarkable not for its length but for who completes it. No single butterfly makes the whole trip from Canada to the forests of central Mexico. Instead the migration is shared across several generations: the butterflies that finally arrive in Mexico are the great-grandchildren of those that first set out. How they find a forest none of them has ever seen remains one of nature's great puzzles.

5C The humpback whale

6Humpback whales swim from the cold, food-rich waters near the poles to warm tropical seas where their calves are born. They make the journey without eating at all, living off the fat they built up over the summer. Along the way the males sing long, complex songs that travel for many kilometres underwater; the same song spreads slowly from group to group until whole oceans seem to share a single tune.

7D The wildebeest

8On the plains of East Africa more than a million wildebeest move in a vast circle, always chasing the rain and the fresh grass it brings. There is no leader; the herd simply turns as one when the weather changes. The most dangerous moments come at the rivers, where crocodiles wait, and only the animals that cross quickly and together are likely to survive.

The River That Came Back to Life
Reading text

1Thirty years ago the River Mell, which runs through the small town of Casterton, was little more than an open drain. Factories upstream had poured waste into it for decades, the water ran a strange grey-green, and the fish had long since disappeared. Most residents simply kept their backs to it.

2The change began with a single retired teacher, Pamela Osei, who started picking litter from the banks on her morning walks. Neighbours who had once ignored the river began to join her, and within a year a small volunteer group had formed. They called themselves the River Watch.

3Their first victory was information. By testing the water themselves and sharing the results online, they put quiet pressure on the factories until two of the worst polluters agreed to install filters. Cleaner water alone, however, was not enough. The group also planted reeds along the banks, which trap dirt and give insects and birds somewhere to shelter, and they removed several old concrete walls so that the river could spread into its natural floodplain after heavy rain.

4Slowly, life returned. Dragonflies appeared first, then small fish, and three years ago an otter — an animal not seen in Casterton for half a century — was photographed at dawn. The town now holds a River Festival every spring, and local schools use the banks as an outdoor classroom.

5Pamela is modest about her part. 'I never set out to start a movement,' she says. 'I just couldn't walk past the mess any more. The lesson, if there is one, is that a place only stays neglected for as long as nobody decides to care for it.'

The Man Who Grows Forests on Rooftops
Reading text

1When Daniel Owusu tells people he is a farmer, they picture open fields. In fact his farms sit on the flat roofs of office blocks and car parks in the centre of one of the busiest cities in West Africa.

2Owusu trained as an architect, not a gardener. For years he designed glass towers like everyone else, until a long illness forced him to spend months indoors looking out over a grey sea of concrete. 'I realised I had helped build a city with almost nowhere for anything green to live,' he says. 'I had been part of the problem.'

3His idea was simple but stubborn: every unused roof, he argued, was a field waiting to happen. Persuading building owners was harder. Many feared the weight of the soil, or leaks, or the cost. Owusu's answer was to start small and let the results speak. He covered one modest rooftop with lightweight beds of vegetables and herbs, tended by people from the neighbourhood, and invited sceptical landlords to come and see.

4The rooftop did more than grow food. In summer the planted roof kept the floor below it noticeably cooler, cutting the building's air-conditioning bills. Rainwater that had once rushed straight into overloaded drains was now soaked up by the soil. Bees and birds, almost unknown in the district, began to return. What had looked like charity turned out to make hard financial sense.

5Today more than forty roofs across the city are under cultivation, and a waiting list of owners want to join. Owusu is careful, though, not to promise too much. 'A few gardens will not undo what cities have done to the natural world,' he warns. 'But they change how people think. Once someone has grown a tomato above the traffic, they never look at a bare roof the same way again. That shift in the mind is the real harvest.'

    8 · Writing

    6a · An informal letter (120–150 words)
    Shape: Where & what → What you saw → Why it matters (+ call to action).   Stems: "You won't believe the ___ I visited…" · "The ___ were so ___ that ___." · "But it's in danger: ___ threatens ___." · "I think we should ___. Write back soon!"
    6b · A conservation report (120–160 words, formal)
    Sections: Current problems → What could be done (passive steps) → Recommendation.   Stems: "For years the river has been ___." · "Several steps could be ___." · "First, the water should be ___." · "It is recommended that ___."
