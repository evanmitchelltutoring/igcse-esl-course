The Shop That Became a Living Room

Reading · core

In Japan they are called konbini; in Thailand, most people just say "the 7-Eleven." Whatever the name, the 24-hour convenience store has quietly turned into something its designers never planned: an unofficial social space for young people.

The reasons start with access. A konbini never closes. While cafés shut and libraries lock their doors, the convenience store stays lit and open through the night, which makes it the one place teenagers can always go. Many branches now add a few seats, a counter by the window, a microwave, an ATM, even a printer — small comforts that turn a quick stop into a place to stay.

Then there is the cost. For a teenager with very little money, the konbini is almost uniquely welcoming: a drink costs small change, and nobody expects you to spend more. "We can sit for an hour with one bottle of tea," says Ken, 16. "No one tells us to leave. At a café you feel the pressure to keep buying."

It has even produced its own habits. In parts of Japan, gathering outside the konbini after school is common enough to be unremarkable — standing around, sharing snacks, and talking. There is no dress code, no booking, and no reason you need to be there except that your friends are.

Not everyone sees it warmly. Some adults view groups of teenagers outside a shop as a nuisance, or worry they are "loitering" rather than buying. A few stores have removed their seating or played music to discourage young people from staying too long. Yet for many owners the calculation is simple: today's teenager drinking cheap tea is tomorrow's regular customer.

What the konbini offers, in the end, is not really products. It is permission — to be somewhere warm and open, for as long as you like, without being asked to spend.
