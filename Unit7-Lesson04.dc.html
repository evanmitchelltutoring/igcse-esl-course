The Business Behind the Team

Mark money · people · careers

A successful esports organisation looks less like a group of friends gaming and more like a small sports business. Behind the five players on stage sits a structure built to find talent, fund it and keep it winning.

Money arrives from several directions. The largest share for most teams is sponsorship — brands pay to place their logos on jerseys and streams. A second source is prize money, won at tournaments and shared between the organisation and its players. A third, fast-growing source is streaming revenue, earned when players broadcast their practice to online audiences who subscribe or donate. Ticket sales from live events add a smaller amount, and selling merchandise such as jerseys and hoodies brings in the rest.

The people involved go far beyond the players. Each team employs a head coach, who designs strategy and reviews matches. An analyst studies opponents and prepares data on their habits. A team manager handles travel, contracts and daily life, while many organisations now add a sports psychologist to protect players' focus and confidence. Larger teams also run an academy — a junior squad where promising young players develop before joining the main roster.

Yet the career is far from secure. Professional play is unusually short: most competitors retire in their mid-twenties, when reaction speeds begin to slow. Rosters change constantly, and a player who underperforms for a few weeks may be dropped. Few earn the headline salaries reported in the news; for every star, many more get by on modest pay and uncertain contracts. Burnout, caused by long practice hours and relentless pressure, ends careers early.

This mix of real money and real risk is part of why supporters insist esports should be taken seriously. The structures — salaries, coaching staff, academies, enforced rules — mirror those of any professional sport. Critics counter that money alone proves nothing: plenty of activities pay well without being called sports.
