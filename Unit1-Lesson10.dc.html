The Corner That Closed

Reading · core

For three years, the small arcade on the corner of Maple Road was where Arif and his friends met after school. It was nothing special — a dozen old machines, a drinks fridge, a torn sofa — but it was warm, it was cheap, and nobody minded if you stayed for hours.

Last month, it closed. The rent had doubled, the owner explained, and a coffee chain had offered to take the space. Now the windows are papered over, and a sign promises a "premium café — opening soon."

Arif and his friends have nowhere obvious to go. The new café, when it opens, will sell coffees none of them can really afford. The mall is a bus ride away. "We're not asking for much," Arif says. "Just somewhere we're allowed to exist without buying something every twenty minutes."

His story is becoming common. Across many cities, the cheap, in-between places where young people gathered — arcades, old cafés, corner shops with a bench outside — are slowly disappearing, replaced by businesses designed for people with money to spend. The spaces that remain often quietly discourage teenagers from lingering.

None of this is dramatic. No one decided to push young people out. It happens one closed shop, one rent rise, one "no loitering" sign at a time. But the result, added up, is a city with fewer and fewer places where being young and having no money is simply allowed.

Skill · the writer's attitude

How does the writer feel about it?

A writer's attitude is how they feel about their subject. They rarely state it — they show it.
Word choice
Warm words or cold ones? "warm, cheap" vs "premium".
What they include
Whose voice do we hear? Whose side gets the last word?
Small signals
Quotation marks, irony, a quiet closing line.
Watch me read the attitude

Two clues, one conclusion

CLUE 1 — a sign promises a "premium café" → the quotation marks keep the writer at a distance; a hint of irony.
CLUE 2 — "places where being young and having no money is simply allowed" → the quiet closing line sides with the young.
CONCLUSION — The writer sympathises with the young people, and is quietly critical of the change — without ever saying so directly.

Check your thinking

1. Nothing special (old machines, a torn sofa) — but warm, cheap, and no one minded if you stayed.
2. The rent doubled and a coffee chain offered to take the space.
3. To keep a distance from the phrase — a hint of irony; it's the owners' word, not the writer's approval.
4. The young people's — shown by "warm", "cheap", "none of them can really afford", "simply allowed".
5. Places where being young and having no money is simply allowed — cheap places to belong.
