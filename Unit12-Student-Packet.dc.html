1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped by strand. Say them, then use them in your writing and speaking.

Rescue & escape
abandon — to leave someone or something behind in danger
beacon — a light or fire used as a warning or signal
current — a continuous flow of water or air in one direction
drift — to be carried along slowly, with no control
evacuate — to move people away from a dangerous place
flare — a bright light fired into the sky as a signal
rescue — to save someone from danger
signal — to send a message or a call for help
survivor — a person who lives through great danger
Danger & terrain
brink — the very edge; the point just before disaster
capsize — (of a boat) to turn over in the water
collapse — to fall down suddenly; to fail completely
jagged — having a rough, sharp, uneven edge
peril — serious and immediate danger
plunge — to fall or drop suddenly and steeply
remote — far away from people, towns or help
summit — the highest point of a mountain
treacherous — dangerous, especially when it looks safe
Mind & resolve
composure — calmness and self-control in a crisis
courage — the ability to face danger, pain or fear
despair — the complete loss of hope
morale — the confidence and spirit of a person or group
panic — a sudden, uncontrollable feeling of fear
willpower — the strength of mind to do something hard
Survival & endurance
dehydrated — having lost too much water from the body
endure — to suffer something difficult patiently
exhaustion — the state of being extremely tired
frostbite — injury to the body caused by extreme cold
persevere — to keep trying despite difficulty
ration — to limit how much you use so it lasts
stranded — left somewhere with no way to leave
Explorer & frontier
ascent — the act of climbing or going up
descent — the act of going down
expedition — an organised journey to somewhere difficult
frontier — the far edge of explored or known land
terrain — an area of land and its physical features
Word families — learn one, gain three
endure → endurance → enduring
explore → exploration → explorer
survive → survival → survivor
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: composure · endure · treacherous.

The reading texts
The Long Walk Out
Reading text

1Maya had been walking for six hours when she finally admitted to herself that she was lost.

2The trail had seemed obvious that morning — a clear line of stones winding up through the pines. But somewhere after the river crossing the path had faded, and the thick mist that rolled down from the peaks had swallowed every landmark she might have used. Now the light was failing, the temperature was dropping fast, and her water bottle was almost empty.

3She made herself stop and think. Panicking, she knew, would only waste energy she could not spare. She remembered the one piece of advice her uncle, an experienced climber, had drummed into her before the trip: if you get lost, do not wander; find water and follow it downhill, because rivers lead to people. It was the only plan she had.

4She found a thin stream within the hour and began to follow it, stumbling over wet rocks in the half-dark. Twice she fell. Her ankle, already sore, began to throb with every step, and there were moments when she wanted nothing more than to sit down in the cold and rest. She did not let herself. If she stopped now, she thought, she might never get up again.

5The stream grew slowly into a noisy little river, and the river, at last, led her to a rough track. Maya could have wept with relief. She turned downhill and followed it until, rounding a bend, she saw the yellow squares of a farmhouse window glowing through the trees. The farmer who opened the door took one look at her — soaked, shaking, white with exhaustion — and pulled her inside without a word.

6Later, wrapped in a blanket with a hot drink in her hands, Maya thought about how close it had been. If she had panicked, if she had sat down to rest, if she had not remembered her uncle's single rule, the night might have ended very differently. She had not done anything heroic, she decided. She had simply kept calm, kept moving, and trusted the water to do the rest.

Twelve Hours in the Cave
Reading text

1When the rain began, Daniel was already half a mile underground.

2He and two friends had gone caving that Saturday, as they often did, following a familiar passage deep beneath the hills. None of them had thought to check the weather. None of them knew that, far above their heads, a sudden storm was turning quiet streams into torrents — or that the water would pour down into the cave system and rise, with terrifying speed, in the narrow tunnels behind them.

3By the time they realised what was happening, the passage they had come in by was already flooded. There was no going back. Daniel made a quick decision and told the others to climb: their only hope was to get higher than the water and wait. They scrambled up onto a narrow ledge, soaked and shivering, and watched the black water rise in the torchlight until it was lapping just below their feet. Then, mercifully, it stopped.

4What followed was the longest night of Daniel's life. The cold seeped into them, and their torches, one by one, began to die, so they switched them off to save the last of the light. To keep everyone's spirits up, Daniel made them talk — about anything, about nothing — because he had read somewhere that the greatest danger in a situation like theirs was not the cold or the water but despair.

5Above ground, their families had raised the alarm, and a cave rescue team was already pulling on its gear. These were volunteers, ordinary people with day jobs, who train for exactly this. It took them hours to reach the trapped cavers, wading and swimming through passages no sensible person would enter, but reach them they did. When the first rescuer's light finally appeared around the bend, Daniel said, none of them could speak; one of his friends simply began to laugh, and could not stop.

6Afterwards, a reporter asked Daniel what he had learned. They had survived, he said, because they had not split up, they had got above the water, and they had kept each other talking through the dark. But the real lesson, he added with a rueful smile, was simpler: next time, he would check the weather first.

Crossing the White
Reading text

1People ask me why anyone would choose to walk across a frozen wilderness, dragging everything they need behind them on a sledge. I am not sure I have ever given a good answer. The truth is that the ice gets into you, and you keep going back.

2My third expedition was the hardest. For five weeks my partner and I hauled our sledges across a great white plain, hundreds of miles from the nearest human being. There is a particular kind of silence out there that I have found nowhere else — no birds, no traffic, no voices, only the hiss of the runners and the sound of your own breathing. Some people find it unbearable. I have come to love it.

3The cold, of course, is the constant enemy. At those temperatures a moment of carelessness — a glove pulled off for too long, a zip left open — can cost you your fingers. Every task that would take seconds at home takes minutes, performed with stiff hands and enormous concentration. You learn to do everything slowly and to check everything twice, because out there a small mistake does not stay small.

4What surprised me most, on that third crossing, was not the hardship but how the mind behaves. There were days when I felt I could walk forever, and days when putting up the tent felt beyond me. I learned that the body will usually do far more than the mind believes, if you can only keep the mind from giving up. On the worst afternoon, when a storm pinned us in the tent for thirty hours, my partner said something I have never forgotten: the secret, she said, was not to think about the whole journey, only about the next hour. Just get through the next hour, and then the next.

5We reached the far coast on the thirty-eighth day, thinner, slower and quieter than we had set out. People imagine a great moment of triumph at the end of such a journey. In truth I mostly felt a deep tiredness, and a strange sadness that it was over. Within a year, of course, I was planning the next one. The ice, as I said, gets into you.

A Night on the Mountain
Reading text

1I have been asked to describe, as clearly as I can, what happened on the mountain that night, while I still remember it well.

2We had set out late — that was our first mistake. By the time we reached the ridge, the afternoon light was already fading, and a cold wind had begun to rise from the valley below. I suggested turning back, but the others wanted to push on to the summit, and I did not argue as firmly as I should have.

3The mist came down without warning. One moment we could see the path ahead; the next, we could see almost nothing. We lost the trail completely. Rather than wander on in the dark, we did the one sensible thing we did all day: we stopped, found a hollow that was sheltered from the wind, and waited for morning. We had no tent, but we had a little food and, luckily, a survival blanket, which we shared between us.

4It was a long, cold night. To stay awake and keep our spirits up, we took turns to talk. Nobody slept much. I will be honest: there was a point, in the small hours, when I was genuinely frightened that one of us might not make it to morning.

5At first light the mist lifted, and we saw, to our astonishment, that we had stopped barely two hundred metres from a marked path. We walked down slowly and were met by a mountain rescue team who had been about to come looking for us. I am told we were very lucky. I believe it.

5 · Grammar (Use of English)

5a · Conditionals

First = real future · second = unreal now · third = regret about the past (had done / would have done).

  1. If you ___ (get) lost, follow water downhill.
  2. If I ___ (be) you, I would turn back now.
  3. If they had checked the forecast, they ___ (not / be) trapped.
5b · Reported speech

Shift the tense back one step and change the pronouns.

  1. "I am turning back," she said. →
  2. "We will wait for morning," they said. →
  3. "The path is dangerous," the guide warned. →

6 · Writing

6a · An email recounting a frightening experience (150–200 words)
Shape (3 beats): Set-up (hook + where + what went wrong) → Crisis (what happened + how you felt; slow the scary moment) → Ending (how it resolved + a lesson).   Warm, informal voice; greeting & sign-off; controlled tenses (had gone · was fading); one "If I had…, I would have…"; target words (stranded · composure · willpower · treacherous · shelter).
6b · A report on a safety issue (150–200 words)
Shape (4 headings): Introduction → The route & its popularity → The dangers → Recommendations.   Neutral, formal voice; recommend with a modal + reason (the council should…, so that…); use the passive (is used by · is exposed); target words (summit · treacherous · stranded · shelter · rescue).
