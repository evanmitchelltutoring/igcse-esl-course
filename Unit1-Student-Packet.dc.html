1 · Vocabulary reference

The 24 core words for this unit. Say them before you meet them; use them in your writing and speaking.

belonging — the feeling of being accepted as part of a group
affordable — cheap enough that most people can pay for it
ambience — the character and atmosphere of a place
linger — to stay somewhere longer because you enjoy it
unwind — to relax after work or stress
cosy — small, warm and comfortable
refuge — a place that gives shelter or escape
convenience — the quality of being easy to use and saving time
hub — a central place where a lot of activity happens
hangout — a place where people go to relax together
ritual — something you do regularly, often with meaning
accessible — easy to reach or use
gather — to come together in one place as a group
amenity — a useful or pleasant facility of a place
footfall — the number of people who enter a place
bustling — full of busy, energetic activity
sprawling — spreading out over a large area
inclusive — open to and welcoming of everyone
sanctuary — a peaceful place of safety and escape
gentrification — when wealthier people move in and price out original residents
displacement — the forced movement of people away from their home
exclusion — the act of keeping someone out of a place or group
communal — shared by, and open to, a whole community
surveillance — close watching of people, especially by cameras
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several words. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

convenient → convenience → conveniently
pollute → pollution → polluted
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: third place · belonging · gentrification.

The reading texts
The Café That Never Asks You to Leave
Reading text

1In Seoul, a "study café" looks nothing like an ordinary coffee shop. There are no sofas and no background chatter — just long desks, soft lamps, a power socket at every seat, and rows of students with headphones on. The idea spread quickly across Asia, and today similar cafés are common in Bangkok, where they fill up fastest during exam season.

2The appeal is simple: space. Many students live in small flats with noisy younger siblings, and school libraries close early. A study café stays open late — some in Bangkok do not lock their doors until midnight — and charges by time rather than by drink. A typical rate is around 60 baht an hour, often with free refills of water, tea or weak coffee.

3Lalita, 17, comes three or four times a week. "At home I get distracted within ten minutes," she says. "Here everyone is working, so I work too. It's quieter than the library, and nobody rushes you." That last point matters. In an ordinary café there is often an unwritten rule — one drink buys you about two hours before a waiter starts hovering. Study cafés remove that pressure completely.

4For owners, the model is steady rather than spectacular. "Students don't spend much on each visit," explains one Bangkok café owner, "but they come back again and again, and they bring their friends." Roughly seven in ten of his customers are under twenty. He keeps the prices low and the wifi fast, and trusts that loyalty will do the rest.

5Not everyone is convinced the trend is healthy. Some teachers worry that paying to study turns concentration into something only wealthier students can afford. Others point out that, for a teenager with nowhere quiet to go, 60 baht for a calm afternoon may be the cheapest, kindest deal in the city.

My Corner
Reading text

1There is a small, slightly crooked table in the back-left corner of the café on Soi 7, and for almost a year now it has been mine.

2In the morning the place is pale and sleepy. Thin grey light leans through the window, the espresso machine clears its throat, and the only sound is the soft tick of someone's laptop keys. By four o'clock everything has changed. The narrow room fills with the warm smell of toasted bread, the low hum of a dozen overlapping conversations, and a golden, dusty light that makes even the chipped cups look beautiful. I like it best then.

3My corner has a wobble in one leg and a long pale scar across the wood, where someone years ago pressed too hard with a pen. The seat is too low and the cushion has gone flat. None of that matters. From there I can see the door, the street, and the old woman who sells flower garlands on the pavement opposite — and nobody can see my screen.

4I come here to read, to wait, to do nothing in particular. The owner no longer asks what I want; he simply nods, and a few minutes later a small, strong, sweetened coffee appears at my elbow. I have never told him my name. He has never told me his. And yet, sliding into that uneven chair with the afternoon noise rising around me, I feel something I never feel at home or at school: I feel, completely and quietly, that I belong here.

The Shop That Became a Living Room
Reading text

1In Japan they are called konbini; in Thailand, most people just say "the 7-Eleven." Whatever the name, the 24-hour convenience store has quietly turned into something its designers never planned: an unofficial social space for young people.

2The reasons start with access. A konbini never closes. While cafés shut and libraries lock their doors, the convenience store stays lit and open through the night, which makes it the one place teenagers can always go. Many branches now add a few seats, a counter by the window, a microwave, an ATM, even a printer — small comforts that turn a quick stop into a place to stay.

3Then there is the cost. For a teenager with very little money, the konbini is almost uniquely welcoming: a drink costs small change, and nobody expects you to spend more. "We can sit for an hour with one bottle of tea," says Ken, 16, who meets his friends at the same store most evenings. "No one tells us to leave. At a café you feel the pressure to keep buying." That freedom from pressure is the quiet heart of the konbini's appeal.

4It has even produced its own habits. In parts of Japan, gathering outside the konbini after school is common enough to be unremarkable — a small daily ritual of standing around, sharing snacks, and talking. The setting is completely ordinary, and that is exactly why it works: there is no dress code, no booking, and no reason you need to be there except that your friends are.

5Not everyone sees it warmly. Some adults view groups of teenagers outside a shop as a nuisance, or worry they are "loitering" rather than buying. A few stores have removed their seating or played music to discourage young people from staying too long. Yet for many shop owners the calculation is simple: today's teenager drinking cheap tea is tomorrow's regular customer, and a steady stream of young people keeps the store busy and alive.

6What the konbini offers, in the end, is not really products. It is permission — to be somewhere warm, lit and free, with the people you choose, at an hour when nowhere else will have you.

The Indoor City
Reading text

1In the hot, rainy cities of Southeast Asia, the shopping mall has become far more than a place to buy things. For many young people it is the default place to spend a free afternoon — and most of them arrive with no intention of shopping at all.

2The first reason is the climate. When the temperature outside climbs past thirty-five degrees, or the afternoon rain arrives without warning, an air-conditioned mall is simply the most comfortable place to be. It costs nothing to walk in, and nobody is turned away at the door.

3That free entry is the second attraction. A café expects you to buy something; a library expects silence; a mall expects neither. A teenager can sit on a bench, watch the crowds, meet friends by the fountain, and stay for hours without spending a single coin. For students with little money, few other spaces are so generous.

4The third and strongest pull is social. Malls have quietly become meeting points — somewhere to see friends, be seen, and feel part of the busy life of the city. The shops are almost a backdrop. Groups gather in food courts not for the food but for the table, the air-conditioning, and each other.

5Malls themselves are divided about this. Crowds of teenagers spending little can crowd out paying customers, and some centres have tried to discourage them — removing seating, or playing music they hope young people will dislike. Yet many owners take the longer view: today's window-shopping teenager is tomorrow's paying adult, and a mall full of life attracts everyone. An empty mall attracts no one.

6So the indoor city endures — not because young people love shopping, but because, in a hot and expensive world, it offers something increasingly rare: a free, cool, welcoming place simply to be.

The Corner That Closed
Reading text

1For three years, the small arcade on the corner of Maple Road was where Arif and his friends met after school. It was nothing special — a dozen old machines, a drinks fridge, a torn sofa — but it was warm, it was cheap, and nobody minded if you stayed for hours.

2Last month, it closed. The rent had doubled, the owner explained, and a coffee chain had offered to take the space. Now the windows are papered over, and a sign promises a "premium café — opening soon."

3Arif and his friends have nowhere obvious to go. The new café, when it opens, will sell coffees none of them can really afford. The mall is a bus ride away. "We're not asking for much," Arif says. "Just somewhere we're allowed to exist without buying something every twenty minutes."

4His story is becoming common. Across many cities, the cheap, in-between places where young people gathered — arcades, old cafés, corner shops with a bench outside — are slowly disappearing, replaced by businesses designed for people with money to spend. The spaces that remain often quietly discourage teenagers from lingering.

5None of this is dramatic. No one decided to push young people out. It happens one closed shop, one rent rise, one "no loitering" sign at a time. But the result, added up, is a city with fewer and fewer places where being young and having no money is simply allowed.

    8 · Writing — a review

    Task

    Write a review of a place where people gather (120–150 words). Reach a clear verdict.

    The five-move review shape
    MoveWhat to do
    1 · VerdictName the place and give your overall opinion.
    2 · DescribeBring the atmosphere alive — use unit words.
    3 · HighlightThe one thing that makes it special.
    4 · CriticismOne honest fault — it makes you believable.
    5 · RecommendWho is it for? End with a recommendation.
    Toolkit
    Stems: Tucked away in ___, ___ is the kind of place where ___. · Its ___ ambience invites you to ___. · What makes it special is ___. · My only criticism is ___. · I'd recommend it to ___.
    Connectives: so · because · however · what's more · most of all.
    Power words: cosy · welcoming · lively · spacious · linger · unwind · sanctuary.
