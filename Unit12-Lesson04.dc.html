Twelve Hours in the Cave

Mark cause & effect

When the rain began, Daniel was already half a mile underground.

He and two friends had gone caving that Saturday, as they often did, following a familiar passage deep beneath the hills. None of them had thought to check the weather. None of them knew that, far above their heads, a sudden storm was turning quiet streams into torrents — or that the water would pour down into the cave system and rise, with terrifying speed, in the narrow tunnels behind them.

By the time they realised what was happening, the passage they had come in by was already flooded. There was no going back. Daniel made a quick decision and told the others to climb: their only hope was to get higher than the water and wait. They scrambled up onto a narrow ledge, soaked and shivering, and watched the black water rise in the torchlight until it was lapping just below their feet. Then, mercifully, it stopped.

What followed was the longest night of Daniel's life. The cold seeped into them, and their torches, one by one, began to die, so they switched them off to save the last of the light. To keep everyone's spirits up, Daniel made them talk — about anything, about nothing — because he had read somewhere that the greatest danger in a situation like theirs was not the cold or the water but despair.

Above ground, their families had raised the alarm, and a cave rescue team was already pulling on its gear. These were volunteers, ordinary people with day jobs, who train for exactly this. It took them hours to reach the trapped cavers, wading and swimming through passages no sensible person would enter, but reach them they did. When the first rescuer's light finally appeared around the bend, Daniel said, none of them could speak; one of his friends simply began to laugh, and could not stop.

Afterwards, a reporter asked Daniel what he had learned. They had survived, he said, because they had not split up, they had got above the water, and they had kept each other talking through the dark. The mistake had been simple — nobody checked the forecast — and it had very nearly cost them everything.
