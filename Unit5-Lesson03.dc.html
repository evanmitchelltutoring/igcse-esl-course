A Picture in Ten Seconds

Read · then read again

In the past, making a detailed picture took training, patience, and hours of work. Today, a new kind of computer program can produce one in about ten seconds. You type a few words — a "prompt", such as a cat reading a book by candlelight — and the program builds an image to match. These programs are a form of artificial intelligence, often shortened to AI.

How do they do it? Each program has studied an enormous collection of pictures, called a dataset, made up of millions of images created by people over many years. From all those examples, the AI learns the patterns that make a picture look a certain way. When you give it a prompt, it does not copy any single picture. Instead, it uses what it has learned to generate something new that fits your words.

The output can be surprising. Some images look like photographs; others mimic the style of famous painters. Much of this work is now automated, which means the machine does most of the steps on its own, with very little help from a person once the prompt is typed.

Public reactions are mixed. Some people are amazed and call the images beautiful. Others are suspicious, because a seemingly original picture might really be built from the work of thousands of artists who were never asked. A third group simply shrugs, treating the whole thing as a passing novelty — fun for now, but not real art.

One thing is certain: the technology is spreading quickly. Students use it for school projects. Businesses use it for advertisements. Artists argue about whether it threatens their jobs or simply gives them a new tool. The pictures may be made in seconds, but the questions they raise will not be settled nearly as fast.
