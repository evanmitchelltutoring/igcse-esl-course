The Roaring Dark

Ring the strongest images

The arena is dark, and then it isn't. Eighteen thousand phone lights bloom at once, a galaxy held in human hands, and the noise that follows hits the chest like a physical thing. On stage, five players sit in a pool of light, hunched over keyboards, utterly still while everything around them erupts. They are about to play a video game. The crowd is about to lose its mind.

If you still picture esports as a hobby for lonely teenagers in darkened bedrooms, this is the scene that should give you pause.

Tickets to events like this sell out in minutes. The biggest finals fill stadiums built for football, and for every fan inside, hundreds of thousands more are watching at home. A single championship can draw millions of concurrent viewers — more, on some nights, than the traditional sports finals shown at the same hour. The audience is not imaginary. It is measurable, vast and growing.

And it behaves exactly as sports crowds always have. People paint their faces in team colours. They gasp as one, groan as one, surge to their feet as one. They queue for merchandise and they weep when their heroes lose. Strip away the screens and the controllers, and what remains is the oldest spectacle in the world: thousands of strangers bound together for a few hours by the fortunes of a team they have chosen to love.

So here is the question the doubters must answer. If a contest fills arenas, sells out broadcasts and moves a crowd to tears, on what grounds is it not a spectator sport? The audience has already decided.

None of this proves that esports demands the body of an Olympic sprinter. But spectacle was never only about the athletes. It was always, also, about us — the watchers, the believers, the ones who show up. And by that measure, esports arrived a long time ago.

Answer · mark your own

Device + effect

1. Metaphor / imagery — "a galaxy" makes the crowd vast, beautiful and awe-inspiring; the spectators feel cosmic, not lonely.
2. Simile — comparing sound to a physical blow makes the atmosphere feel real and bodily, overwhelming the reader as it does the fan.
3. Repetition / list ("as one") — shows the crowd behaving exactly like a traditional sports crowd, building the case that it is a spectator sport.
4. Rhetorical question — puts the burden on the doubters and makes their objection sound weak against the evidence of the crowd.
5. It concedes the crowd doesn't prove gamers have an Olympic body — then reframes "sport" as spectacle, which is about the watchers. Conceding makes the argument sound fair.
