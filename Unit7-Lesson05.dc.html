Bodies Pushed Hard

Mark demands · costs · responses

Ask whether professional gamers are athletes and you will start an argument. Look closely at what their bodies actually do, however, and the question becomes harder to dismiss.

The clearest physical demand is speed. Top players register reaction times far faster than the average adult, responding to events on screen in fractions of a second. In some games they perform several hundred deliberate actions every minute — a rate of precise hand and finger movement that few activities require. Holding that accuracy for hours, match after match, calls for fine motor control and intense concentration rather than brute strength.

This precision comes at a cost. Years of rapid, repeated movements can cause repetitive strain injuries in the wrists and hands, ending careers as surely as a torn muscle ends a runner's. Long hours seated bring back and neck problems, and constant focus on a bright screen tires the eyes. The body is not driven to exhaustion the way a sprinter's is, but it is pushed hard in its own narrow way.

Teams have responded much as traditional clubs do. Many now employ sports scientists to measure performance and recovery. Physiotherapists treat and prevent the strain injuries that threaten careers. Nutritionists plan meals to keep energy and focus steady, and some organisations enforce fitness routines, arguing that a fitter body supports a sharper mind. Sleep, once ignored, is now managed carefully.

Whether all this makes a gamer an athlete depends on what the word means. If an athlete is someone whose body must be trained, protected and pushed to compete, professional players have a case. If the word demands large-scale physical exertion, the case is weaker. What is not in doubt is that the body matters.
