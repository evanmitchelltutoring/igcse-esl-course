Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several. Learn the family and you always have the right form for a sentence.

adapt → adaptable → adaptability
motivate → motivation → motivated

First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: self-directed · obsolete · perseverance.

The reading texts
Teaching Myself, One Tab at a Time
Reading text

1When Maya decided she wanted to make her own animated short films, she did not sign up for a class. There were no animation courses at her school, and the nearest studio was a two-hour bus ride away. Instead, she opened her laptop, typed 'how to animate for beginners' into a search bar, and pressed enter.

2That single search led her to thousands of free tutorials. Some were excellent; many were not. At first the sheer number of choices was overwhelming. Maya quickly learned that being a self-directed learner meant more than just watching videos — it meant deciding which videos were worth her time. She became resourceful, testing a technique for ten minutes and abandoning it the moment it led nowhere.

3Progress was slow. Her first animation lasted four seconds and took a week to make. But every small success gave her the motivation to continue. She kept a simple notebook in which she wrote down what had gone wrong and what she would try next. Looking back, she says this habit of reflection mattered more than any single tutorial.

4What surprised Maya most was how much her curiosity grew the more she learned. A question about movement led her to study how real animals walk; a question about colour led her to read about light. One skill opened a door to another. Within a year she had acquired a level of proficiency that astonished her teachers — none of whom had taught her a thing.

5Maya is not unusual. Across the world, teenagers are teaching themselves skills that were once locked inside expensive institutions. The tools are now accessible to almost anyone with a phone and a little patience. Coding, cooking, music production, three languages at once — the only real entry requirement is the initiative to begin.

6Of course, learning alone has its dangers. Without a teacher to correct them, self-taught learners can practise their mistakes until those mistakes start to feel normal. Maya admits she spent months animating in a way she later had to unlearn. 'No one told me I was doing it wrong,' she says, 'because no one was watching.' She solved this by posting her work online and asking strangers for honest opinions — proof that even the most autonomous learner needs other people in the end.

7Today Maya mentors younger students who want to start. Her advice is never about software. 'Pick something tiny,' she tells them. 'Finish it. Then pick something slightly bigger.' Learning without a classroom, she insists, is less about talent than about the quiet decision to keep going when no one is making you.

The People Who Helped Me Learn
Reading text

1Learning rarely happens entirely alone. We asked four people how a community — online or face to face — changed the way they learn.

2A — TOMAS, 17

3I taught myself to code, but I would have given up without an online forum I joined. Whenever I got stuck, I posted a question and someone usually answered within minutes. What I valued most was the speed: I could keep moving instead of staring at the same error for hours. Over time I stopped only asking questions and started answering other beginners. Strangely, explaining things to other people taught me more than any tutorial ever did.

4B — AISHA, 16

5My community is not online at all. Every Saturday I go to a sewing circle at the local hall, where most of the members are older than my grandmother. They have decades of expertise in their hands, and they pass it on without any fuss. There is no screen, no app — just someone leaning over and gently showing me where I went wrong. The encouragement from women who have been making clothes for fifty years is worth more to me than a hundred videos.

6C — LENA, 18

7I use a language-exchange app that pairs me with a student in another country. I help her with English; she helps me with Spanish. Neither of us is a teacher, so we are equals — just two learners helping each other. Because we both gain something, neither of us feels embarrassed about mistakes. I think learning with a peer who needs you as much as you need them removes most of the fear.

8D — SAM, 17

9Honestly, I nearly quit studying for my exams. What saved me was a small study group on a messaging app. We set goals together and checked on each other every evening. Knowing that four other people would notice if I disappeared kept me showing up. The subject mattered far less than the simple fact that I was no longer doing it alone. That kind of support is the only reason I'm still going.

10All four describe something the same: a community does not just deliver information. It supplies the guidance, the encouragement and the steady accountability that keep a learner moving when motivation alone runs out.

The Most Useful Thing You'll Get Wrong
Reading text

1Most people treat a mistake as something to feel ashamed of and forget as quickly as possible. Yet the people who improve fastest do almost the opposite: they slow down, study the mistake, and squeeze every lesson out of it. For them, an error is not a verdict on their ability. It is information.

2Consider how a child learns to walk. They fall hundreds of times, but no one calls a falling baby a failure. Each stumble quietly adjusts their balance for the next attempt. Somewhere on the road to adulthood, many of us lose this attitude. We begin to see mistakes as proof that we are not clever enough, and so we hide them. The problem is that a mistake you hide is a mistake you cannot learn from.

3The first step, then, is simply to look. When you get something wrong, resist the urge to move on. Instead, analyse it: ask exactly where your thinking went astray and why. A student who only notices that an answer was wrong learns very little; a student who works out the reason rarely repeats the error.

4The second step is to invite other eyes. We are often blind to our own habits, and honest, constructive feedback can reveal a weakness we have walked past a hundred times. Feedback can sting, but the discomfort fades far faster than the cost of repeating the same mistake for years.

5The third step is reflection. After feedback, take a quiet moment to ask what you will do differently. This is not vague worrying; it is a deliberate plan. Many successful learners keep a record of their errors precisely so that patterns become visible over time.

6None of this is comfortable, which is why resilience matters so much. Studying your own mistakes means accepting, again and again, that you are not yet as good as you would like to be. That takes a certain determination — a willingness to be a beginner long after you wish you had finished. The reward is that each setback becomes a step rather than a wall.

7It helps to remember that the alternative is worse. People who never risk being wrong never stretch beyond what they can already do. They protect a small, safe circle of skill and call it competence. Real growth lives just outside that circle, in the messy territory where things go wrong — and where, if you are paying attention, you learn the most.

8So the next time you get something badly wrong, try to feel a flicker of curiosity rather than only frustration. Somewhere inside that mistake is the exact instruction you needed.

You Will Never Finish Learning
Reading text

1There was a time when education had a finish line. You learned a trade or a profession, crossed that line once, and coasted on the same knowledge for the rest of your working life. That world is gone, and it is not coming back.

2Today, skills have a shelf life. A tool that defines an entire industry can be born, dominate, and slide into the dustbin of the obsolete within a single decade. The ground beneath every career is quietly shifting, and standing still — once the safest thing you could do — has become the most dangerous.

3This sounds exhausting, and it can be. But there is another way to see it. If learning never ends, then you are never too late, never finished, never trapped. The same churn that threatens the person who refuses to grow becomes an open door for the person who is willing to. Change does not only take things away; it hands out fresh chances to anyone prepared to reach for them.

4The skill that matters most now is not any single skill at all. It is the ability to learn quickly, to adapt, and to begin again without shame. A versatile mind that can move between fields will always outlast a brilliant one frozen in place. We used to admire the expert who knew one thing completely. Increasingly, we will admire the learner who can become a beginner over and over without flinching.

5None of this means your education was wasted. Think of it instead as a foundation rather than a finished house — something solid to build upon, not something to live inside forever. Every new thing you learn widens the ground you can stand on, and a wider foundation makes the next thing easier to add.

6There will always be a voice that whispers you are too old, too busy, too far behind. Ignore it. The grandmother learning to code, the taxi driver studying law at midnight, the teenager teaching themselves a language one phone screen at a time — they have all made the same quiet decision. They have decided that the world may keep changing, but they are not going to let it leave them behind.

7So do not mourn the finish line that no longer exists. Celebrate it. A race with no end is also a race you can never truly lose — as long as you keep moving.

have/has + been + -ing; for + a length, since + a start point.

6 · Writing

6a · An informal email — a study spot I found (120–160 words)
Shape: Greeting → the spot (why you like it) → how it's helped → invite with a when → warm sign-off.   Friendly register; a gerund ("I love working there") and a present perfect continuous ("I've been studying there"); target words (accessible · motivation · self-directed · supportive · milestone).
6b · An argumentative essay — "Everyone should keep learning for life." (120–160 words)
Shape: Position → 2× P-R-R-E (Point → Reason → Result → Example) → Conclusion that looks forward.   Hold one position; use a gerund and a present perfect continuous; target phrases (stay relevant · skills become obsolete · adapt to a changing world · a growth mindset · reach our potential).
