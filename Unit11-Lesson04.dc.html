What the Room Told the Detective

Mark each clue & what it rules out

Detective Sarah Okoro stood in the doorway of the study and did not move. The good ones never rushed in; they read the room first, the way you read a page.

The window was open, but the flowerbed beneath it was undisturbed — no footprints, no broken stems. Whoever had taken the painting, she thought, had not come in that way. On the desk, a cup of coffee had been left half-finished. She touched the side of the cup. It was still faintly warm. Someone had been here within the hour.

The safe in the corner stood open and empty. It had not been forced; there was not a scratch on the lock. Either it had been left open, which seemed unlikely for so careful a man as the owner, or someone had known the code. Three people knew it: the owner, his brother, and the housekeeper.

On the carpet lay a single dark thread, the kind from an expensive coat. The owner had been away in another city since the night before, and his train ticket was still in his coat pocket upstairs. So he could not have stood in this room that morning. That left two people.

The housekeeper had a key but, by her own account, had not entered the study for days. Yet the coffee was fresh, and the housekeeper was the only person in the house who made coffee. Okoro did not announce her conclusion. She simply asked, in her gentlest voice, whether she might trouble the housekeeper for a fresh cup of coffee — and watched, very carefully, to see how the woman's hands would move.

What each clue implies

1. No one came in through the window — the flowerbed was "undisturbed".
2. Someone was there within the hour — the cup was "still faintly warm".
3. No scratch → not forced → someone knew the code: owner, brother, or housekeeper.
4. He was away since the night before; his train ticket was upstairs.
5. She tests the housekeeper — the only coffee-maker — watching for a nervous reaction.

The evidence points to the housekeeper — but the story leaves the proof to the next moment.
