Unit 11 · Mystery & the Unexplained

Clues, deduction, hoaxes — the mystery story and the report.

This is the Student Packet plus everything you need to teach and mark Unit 11: answer keys (red callouts) after every task, a "Watch for" misconception box, per-lesson pacing, a Support / Stretch line, the writing mark scheme, and the assessment-objective map. Before you print AO tags — confirmThe AO wording below follows Cambridge IGCSE ESL 0510 / 0511 (9-1 = 0991 / 0993). Confirm the exact syllabus code and current AO wording against the live syllabus before printing tags in student-facing materials.
Tone — mystery, not fear (all lessons)The unit uses eerie, atmospheric stories, but the aim is analytical: read the clues, weigh the evidence, spot the hoax. Keep it intriguing rather than frightening; if a student is uneasy with a "spooky" topic, let them pick a puzzle or unsolved-history angle instead. The through-line is judgement, not belief in the supernatural.
Listening — no audio files (L8)There are no recordings. Read each of the four scripts aloud, twice, at a steady exam pace (or cast confident students in the interview roles). Full scripts are in the speaker-notes of the L8 task slides.

The reading texts
The Vanishing Village
Reading text

1In 1930, a supply ship arrived at a remote island off the coast of Scotland to relieve the three men who kept watch over its small settlement of sheep farmers and their families. The crew expected the usual welcome: smoke rising from chimneys, dogs barking on the shore, someone waving from the jetty. Instead, they found nothing. The village of nineteen houses stood silent in the mist.

2The crew went ashore and searched from house to house. Fires had been laid in several grates but never lit. A half-eaten meal sat on a table in one cottage, the chair pushed back as though someone had risen in a hurry. In another house, a Bible lay open on a windowsill, a knife resting across its pages to mark the place. Clothes hung on lines outside, still damp from washing. There was no sign of struggle anywhere, and no sign of the thirty-six villagers who had lived there only weeks before.

3The last letter sent from the island, found half-written at a desk, mentioned nothing unusual - only a note about the weather turning and a plan to mend a boat before winter. The islanders' small fishing boats were still tied up at the jetty, which ruled out the simplest explanation: that they had all left together by sea.

4Over the following decades, theories multiplied. Some said a sudden storm must have driven the villagers to seek shelter on a neighbouring island, though no record of their arrival anywhere was ever found. Others suggested a disease had frightened them into fleeing overland, although the island's cliffs made such a journey almost impossible on foot. A few, only half joking, wondered whether the villagers had simply grown tired of watching for ships that so rarely came, and vanished by a route no one thought to search.

5What is agreed is this: the meal was left unfinished, the fires unlit, and the Bible marked at a page no one would ever read again. To this day, no bone, no boat, and no letter has ever explained what became of the people of that island.

What the Room Told the Detective
Reading text

1Detective Sarah Okoro stood in the doorway of the study and did not move. The good ones never rushed in; they read the room first, the way you read a page.

2The window was open, letting in the cold morning air, but the flowerbed beneath it was undisturbed — no footprints, no broken stems. Whoever had taken the painting, she thought, had not come in that way. On the desk, a cup of coffee had been left half-finished. She touched the side of the cup. It was still faintly warm. Someone had been here within the hour.

3The safe in the corner stood open and empty. It had not been forced; there was not a scratch on the lock. Either it had been left open, which seemed unlikely for so careful a man as the owner, or someone had known the code. Three people knew it, she had been told: the owner, his brother, and the housekeeper.

4On the carpet near the safe lay a single dark thread, the kind from an expensive coat. The owner, she remembered, had been away in another city since the night before, and his train ticket was still in his coat pocket upstairs. So he could not have stood in this room that morning. That left two people.

5The housekeeper had a key but, by her own account, had not entered the study for days. Yet the coffee was fresh, and the housekeeper was the only person in the house who made coffee. Okoro turned the thought over carefully. She did not yet have proof, but she had something better than a guess: a set of small facts that, fitted together, pointed in one clear direction.

6She did not announce her conclusion. A good detective, she had learned long ago, keeps her theory quiet until the evidence is ready to speak for itself. She simply asked, in her gentlest voice, whether she might trouble the housekeeper for a fresh cup of coffee, and watched, very carefully, to see how the woman's hands would move.

Hoax or Real?
Reading text

1Every few years, a photograph appears that seems to show something impossible: a strange creature in a lake, a light in the sky, a figure that should not be there. The pictures spread quickly, and for a while everyone argues. Then, often, the truth comes out, and it is rarely as exciting as the photograph.

2So how can you tell a hoax from something real? The first question to ask is not whether the thing is amazing, but who is telling you, and how they know. A claim is only as strong as its source. A blurred photo posted by an unknown account, with no date and no location, is weak evidence, however striking it looks. A clear report from several independent witnesses, who do not know each other and have nothing to gain, is far stronger.

3The second test is whether the claim can be checked. Real evidence can be examined by other people: a photograph can be studied for signs of editing, a footprint can be measured and compared. A hoax usually cannot survive this. Take the famous lake-monster photograph of the last century, which thrilled the world for decades. It showed a long neck rising from the water. Many years later, one of the men involved confessed: the monster had been a toy, a small model fixed to a floating wooden base and photographed close up. The picture had never been what it seemed.

4A third warning sign is the story that fits a little too neatly. Hoaxers know what people want to see, and they give it to them. When a claim arrives perfectly formed, with no loose ends and no awkward details, a careful thinker becomes more suspicious, not less. Real events are usually messy; they leave questions behind.

5None of this means you should believe nothing. Some genuine discoveries did, at first, seem impossible. The point is not to switch off your curiosity, but to switch on your judgement. Ask who is speaking, ask whether the claim can be tested, and be wary of the answer that is simply too good. A real mystery can stand up to questions. A hoax falls apart the moment you start asking them.

An Eyewitness Account
Reading text

1I have been asked to write down exactly what I saw on the night of the fourteenth, while it is still fresh in my memory.

2It was just after eleven o'clock. I could not sleep, so I was standing at my bedroom window, which looks out over the square. The street was empty and very quiet, and a light rain was falling.

3That was when I noticed the van. It was parked outside the jeweller's shop on the far corner, with its lights off, which struck me as odd at that hour. I am fairly sure it was dark blue, though the street lamp nearest it was broken, so I cannot be completely certain about the colour.

4After a minute or two, two people got out. Both wore dark coats with the hoods up, so I could not see their faces at all. One of them was noticeably taller than the other. The shorter one carried a bag of some kind, quite large and square. They walked quickly to the shop door and stood there with their backs to me for perhaps thirty seconds. I did not hear any glass break.

5Then a car came into the square from the south, and its headlights swept across the corner. The moment the light touched them, both figures turned and walked away, unhurried, in opposite directions. The van stayed where it was. I did not see anyone drive it away, but when I looked again at about half past eleven, it had gone.

6I want to be honest about what I am not sure of. I never saw either person's face. I cannot swear to the colour of the van. And I cannot say for certain that any crime took place at all — only that what I saw seemed strange enough to report.

4c · "Hoax or Real?" — fact or claim
Answer key · 4cno-name account = F · "obviously a real monster" = C · shown to be a toy = F · independent witnesses agree = F · "anyone can see it proves…" = C. Red-flag claim words: obviously, definitely, anyone can see, it proves.
4d · "An Eyewitness Account" — reliability
Answer key · 4d1 Clearly saw: two people got out · one taller · they left when headlights swept across.   2 Not sure: the van's colour ("fairly sure… cannot be completely certain") — accept "never saw their faces".   3 Admitting gaps shows she isn't overstating, so we trust the rest more. 6 · Writing

6a · Mystery story (Set-up → Strange event → Reflection)
Suspense is control: three beats; must include the door locked then found open; one detail that can't be explained; at least one suspense move (slow the moment, hold detail back, use a sense); controlled tenses. Target words used naturally. 150–200 words. Full model ("The Door") in L9.
6b · Report on a strange event (Intro → Claims → Reliability → Conclusion)
Describe, weigh, don't overclaim: headings; neutral formal voice; every claim attributed; reliability weighed with might/can't/must have; one ordinary explanation; honest conclusion. Target words. 150–200 words. Full model ("The Reported Lights over Hillford") in L10.

9b · "Watch for" — misconceptions specific to this unit
Clue vs guess (L1, L4)Students state a theory as if it were a fact ("the housekeeper did it"). Drill: a clue is what you can point to; a theory is what you think about it. Say "the evidence suggests…" for a fact, "it seems likely that…" for a guess.
Inference (L4)Inferring beyond the text, or naming a clue with no inference. Require both: the detail AND what it lets you work out.
Fact vs claim (L5)Treating a confident claim as a fact. Drill the test: could someone else check this? Flag "obviously / definitely / anyone can see".
Short answers (L3)Over-writing. Part 1 wants the fewest correct words; over the limit or wrong extra detail can lose the mark.
Narrative tenses & deduction (L7)Flat past simple with no past perfect for the earlier event; wrong modal for certainty ("might have" where the clue proves it). Drill: had done for earlier; match modal to how sure.
Story vs report (L9 vs L10)Story with no suspense (plain "and then"); report that turns dramatic or overclaims. Story = three beats + a suspense move; report = neutral, attributed, honest conclusion.
Listening signals (L8)Writing a rumour down as a fact. Train the ear for "reportedly / it is said" (rumour) vs "most believe / probably" (doubt) vs a plainly-stated dated fact.
Debate (L12)Flat contradiction, or asserting the supernatural. Require a reason or example each turn, and end by naming what would change your mind.
9c · Support / Stretch
Support — give the frames: "The clue that ___ suggests ___, though there's no proof." · "Because ___, they must / might / can't have ___." · "Witnesses reported ___; however, ___." Word bank: clue · evidence · deduce · eerie · hoax · testimony. Provide the story/report skeleton pre-labelled so students only fill content.
Stretch — in the story, plant a clue early and pay it off; chain a past-perfect setup with a deduction modal; in the report, weigh two ordinary explanations and choose; in the debate, concede the strongest opposing point, not the weakest.

Listening · answers & transcripts
Voices on the Unexplained — the Vanishing Village
Answers
1 An empty village with no signs of what happened  ·  2 No official records or contemporary reputable news accounts confirming it  ·  3 A — that it is likely exaggerated or invented  ·  4 That unverified stories can be repeated as fact for a long time regardless of truth  ·  5 B — 1930.  ·  6 B — About thirty.  ·  7 C — A team of sled dogs.  ·  8 D — They were still smouldering.
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

NARRATOR: In the winter of 1930, the small farming village of Angikuni, in a remote part of northern Canada, was reportedly found completely empty. According to the story, a fur trapper passed through and discovered cooking fires still smouldering, food left half-eaten, and even a team of sled dogs found starved to death, still tethered outside empty homes. Every single resident — reportedly around thirty people — had vanished without a trace.

NARRATOR: The story spread quickly through newspapers at the time, and search parties were said to have combed the surrounding wilderness for weeks, finding no bodies, no signs of struggle, and no explanation.

NARRATOR: But here's where the story gets complicated. Historians and journalists who later investigated found no official government or police records confirming a search ever took place, and no contemporary newspaper accounts from reputable outlets at the time the disappearance supposedly occurred. Most researchers now believe the story was either heavily exaggerated from a much smaller, explainable incident, or invented outright by a later writer looking for a dramatic headline.

NARRATOR: Whatever really happened at Angikuni — whether it was a genuine mystery, a misunderstanding, or simply a myth — the story remains a striking example of how quickly an unverified tale can take on a life of its own, repeated as fact for nearly a century.

Voices on the Unexplained — the Ghost Ship Mary Celeste
Answers
1 A — 1872  ·  2 B — Food and water still in the galley  ·  3 C — The lifeboat  ·  4 A — The crew panicked over fumes and abandoned ship, then became stranded  ·  5 B — It has never been confirmed.  ·  6 D — Ten.  ·  7 C — The captain's wife and daughter.  ·  8 A — Alcohol.
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

NARRATOR: In December 1872, a merchant ship called the Mary Celeste was discovered adrift in the Atlantic Ocean, sailing under partial sail with nobody on board. The ship was in good condition, its cargo mostly untouched, and there was still food and water in the galley. Yet the entire crew, along with the captain's wife and young daughter, had vanished.

NARRATOR: There was no sign of violence, no note, and the ship's lifeboat was missing, suggesting the crew had left deliberately, perhaps in a hurry. What could have caused ten people to abandon a perfectly seaworthy vessel in the middle of the ocean?

NARRATOR: Over the years, dozens of theories have been proposed, from mutiny and piracy to sea monsters and alien abduction. However, most modern maritime historians favour a far less dramatic explanation: that the crew, fearing an explosion from fumes leaking out of the alcohol cargo they were carrying, panicked and abandoned ship in the lifeboat, only for a sudden storm or a snapped tow-rope to separate them from the Mary Celeste, leaving them stranded and lost at sea.

NARRATOR: The true fate of the crew has never been confirmed, and the Mary Celeste remains one of the most famous maritime mysteries in history, still discussed by historians and sailors alike more than 150 years later.

Speaking · assessment
Speaking · Speaking — A mystery that fascinates you
RefCriterionWhat earns the mark
Model response
The mystery that fascinates me most is a ship that was once found drifting in calm seas with no one on board. What makes it so baffling is that everything was still in place — the table was set and the lamps were burning low, and yet the whole crew had simply vanished. I first read about it in an old book when I was about thirteen, and I remember staying up far too late, completely gripped by the suspense of it. It still fills me with a strange unease, because no theory quite fits the evidence. People say the crew must have panicked and abandoned ship, but if the sea was calm, why would they? It can't have been a storm, and there was no sign of an attack. My own best guess is that something frightened them — a leak they misjudged, perhaps — but honestly, the most likely explanation is one we will never prove. More than anything, I'd love to know what those final hours were really like. For me, it's a reminder that some questions are more interesting than their answers.
