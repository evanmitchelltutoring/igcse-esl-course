The Highlight Reel

Read · then read again

Aisha Karim used to measure her days against a screen. Each evening she would scroll through photographs of classmates laughing on perfect beaches, winning competitions, and seemingly never having a bad hair day. By the time she put her phone down, her own life felt grey by comparison. 'I wasn't sad about anything real,' she remembers. 'I was sad that I wasn't someone else.'

What Aisha did not yet understand is something psychologists have studied for years. Social media shows us other people's 'highlight reels' — the carefully chosen best moments — while we experience our own lives in full, including the boredom, the worry and the ordinary afternoons. Comparing the two is not just unfair; it is impossible to win.

The turning point for Aisha was small. A teacher mentioned, almost in passing, that the feeds we scroll are 'edited, not lived'. The phrase stuck. Aisha began to notice how often a single post could shift her whole mood, and how rarely that shift had anything to do with her actual day.

She did not delete her accounts — she is quick to say she is not against social media. Instead she ran an experiment. For one month she unfollowed any account that left her feeling worse, and started following ones that taught her something or made her laugh. She also set a rule: no scrolling for the first hour after waking, when, she had noticed, her mood was most easily knocked.

The change was not dramatic, but it was real. 'I didn't suddenly love my life,' she says. 'I just stopped renting space in my head to strangers.' Her concentration improved, and so, slowly, did her sleep.

Aisha now helps run a peer-support group at her school, where students talk openly about the pressure of comparison. She is careful not to preach. The point, she insists, is that a feed is a choice, not a mirror — and that noticing how something makes you feel is the first, quietest step towards change.
