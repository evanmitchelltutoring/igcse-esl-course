Clinic 1 · Command words

The trap: answering the wrong question

Under pressure, students answer the question they expected, not the one printed. They "describe" when it says "give two reasons", or copy a sentence when it says "in your own words" — and lose marks they clearly knew.
Command words decide the shape of your answer
Give / State — a short, exact fact. No explanation.
Explain / Why — a reason, not just the fact.
In your own words — you must reword; lifting scores zero.
How does the writer… — name the method + its effect.
The fix · underline before you answer

Circle the command word + the marks first

The marks tell you how many points to make: [2] means two separate details. Never write a paragraph where two words would score.
Rep · what is each one really asking?
1. "Give two reasons the library nearly closed. [2]" → two facts / one? explain?
2. "In your own words, explain how Maria felt. [2]" → reword / lift?
3. "How does the writer show the town cared? [3]" → method + effect / just a fact?
Answers: 1 · two separate facts, no explaining. 2 · reword — lifting scores zero. 3 · name the method (e.g. the detail of 200 people) and its effect.
Clinic 2 · Skimming & scanning

Don't read every word — hunt

The trap
Reading top-to-bottom, word by word, and running out of time before the last passage. The paper is not a race to read — it's a hunt for specific facts.
The fix · two speeds
Skim once for shape — what each paragraph is broadly about. Then scan for the exact word from the question (a name, a number, a place) and read only around it.
Key move: take the unusual word from the question ("midnight", "four hundred") and let your eye chase only that down the page.
Rep · scan, don't read · "The Library That Would Not Close"

Find four facts as fast as you can

Riverside Public Library had served the town for sixty years, but last spring the council announced funding had run out and the building would shut by the end of June. Maria Torres, the head librarian, had worked there since she was twenty-two, and refused to accept the doors would lock for good. Within a week she organised a public meeting; over two hundred people crowded in. The council gave the group just ten weeks to prove the new model could work. By September, membership had passed four hundred, well above target. Maria now spends one afternoon a month mentoring librarians from three neighbouring towns.
1. How long had the library served the town? · 2. How many came to the meeting? · 3. How many weeks were they given? · 4. What membership did it pass by September?
Answers: 1 · sixty years · 2 · over two hundred · 3 · ten weeks · 4 · four hundred. Did you scan for the numbers — or read the whole thing?
Clinic 3 · Matching questions

Spot the trap option

The trap
A distractor reuses a word from the text but changes the meaning. "The friendships were the part I never expected" is not the same as "made friends easily" — one is a surprise, the other a skill.
The fix · match meaning
Read the whole account first, sum up its point in your own words, then find the option that means that. Never pick because a word looks familiar.
Cross off as you go: once an option is used, strike it out so a distractor can't tempt you twice.
Rep · match meaning · "Four Volunteers, Four Journeys"

Who is it? Match on meaning, not words

Mei — taught computer skills; her students were nervous, not slow. Carlos — beach clean-up; the friendships surprised him most.
Priya — tutors teens; learned encouragement beats correction. Tom — delivers groceries; the conversation mattered more than the food.
1. Who valued the relationships more than the task itself? ___   (trap: two of them mention people!)
2. Who changed their teaching approach? ___
3. Who had a wrong first assumption corrected? ___
Answers: 1 · Carlos and Tom both value people — but Q1's "more than the task" points to Carlos (friendships) or Tom (conversation over groceries); read the exact wording. 2 · Priya. 3 · Mei (nervous, not slow).
Clinic 4 · Inference vs explicit

Some answers are shown, not stated

The trap
Hunting for a sentence that states the answer outright — when the paper wants you to work it out from what a character does. Or the opposite: inventing a feeling the text doesn't support.
The fix · show me the evidence
Find the action or detail, ask "so what does that tell me?", and check the words support it. "He asked her to sit next to him" implies approval — the evidence is right there.
Test yourself: could you point to the exact words behind your inference? If not, it's a guess — reject it.
Rep · explicit or inferred? · "First Day at the Newsdesk"

Answer — and quote the evidence

Amara arrived forty minutes early on her first day of work experience. A reporter, Felix, handed her council documents and said, "Find me a story in there by lunch." She found a buried proposal to close the only free youth centre. When she showed Felix her notes, he read them without speaking, then said, "This is a real story." He did not say she had done well, exactly, but he asked her to sit at the desk next to his for the rest of the week — which she took as a much stronger compliment.
1. How early did Amara arrive? explicit / inferred?
2. Was Amara keen to do well? explicit / inferred?
3. Did Felix think she had done well? explicit / inferred — evidence?
Answers: 1 · explicit — "forty minutes early". 2 · inferred — arriving early "to control" the day. 3 · inferred — he never says so, but "asked her to sit next to him… a much stronger compliment" is the evidence.
Clinic 5 · Time allocation

Build a battle plan

The trap
Perfecting the easy 1-mark questions early, then rushing the big summary worth far more. Marks per minute collapse at the exact point they should peak.
The fix · minutes follow marks
Give each section time in proportion to its marks, set a hard stop for each, and if you're stuck, leave it and move on. A blank you return to beats a rushed paper.
Reserve the last 5 minutes to transfer answers and check nothing is blank — never for one more hard question.

Take it into the exam

Five moves for the Reading paper

1Circle the command word and the marks before you answer.
2Skim for shape, then scan for the exact word — don't read every line.
3In matching, match on meaning — and cross off options as you use them.
4For inference, point to the exact words — no evidence, no answer.
5Minutes follow marks; keep the last five for checking.
