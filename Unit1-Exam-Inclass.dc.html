The text (for reference)

In the hot, rainy cities of Southeast Asia, the shopping mall has become far more than a place to buy things. For many young people it is the default place to spend a free afternoon — and most of them arrive with no intention of shopping at all. The first reason is the climate: when the temperature climbs past thirty-five degrees, or the afternoon rain arrives, an air-conditioned mall is simply the most comfortable place to be. It costs nothing to walk in, and nobody is turned away. That free entry is the second attraction. A café expects you to buy something; a library expects silence; a mall expects neither. A teenager can sit on a bench, watch the crowds, meet friends by the fountain, and stay for hours without spending a single coin. The third and strongest pull is social. Malls have quietly become meeting points — somewhere to see friends, be seen, and feel part of the busy life of the city; the shops are almost a backdrop. Malls themselves are divided: crowds of teenagers spending little can crowd out paying customers, and some centres remove seating or play music to discourage them. Yet many owners take the longer view: today's window-shopping teenager is tomorrow's paying adult, and a mall full of life attracts everyone. So the indoor city endures — not because young people love shopping, but because, in a hot and expensive world, it offers something increasingly rare: a free, cool, welcoming place simply to be.
