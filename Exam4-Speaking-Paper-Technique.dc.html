Clinic 1 · The warm-up

Not assessed — but it still matters

The trap
Treating the warm-up as a throwaway — one-word answers, eyes down — then meeting the card cold, nervous, and still finding your voice on the part that counts.
The fix · use it to settle
Answer in a sentence or two, look up, and let your voice warm. It's not assessed, so it costs nothing — but it sets your nerves and your pace for the card. If you're nervous, say so.
By the time the card appears, you want to already be talking comfortably — not starting from silence.
Rep · in pairs · A talks, B listens · then swap

Answer in a sentence or two — and look up

"Tell me a little about where you live and who you live with."
"What are you studying, and which subject do you enjoy most?"
"What do you usually do after school or at the weekend?"
Listener's checklist: more than one word? · a reason added? · looked up, not down? Give one thing that worked and one to grow.
Clinic 2 · Reading the topic card

The prompts are a ladder, not a list

The trap
Marching through all five prompts with a sentence each — or writing full sentences in the prep time and reading them aloud, which the examiner hears instantly.
The fix · climb one prompt
You don't have to use them all. If one prompt opens into something you actually want to say, follow it and let the others go. Jot brief notes — words, not sentences.
Two or three prompts explored deeply beat five prompts touched once. Depth is what earns the development mark.
Rep · topic card · Technology & everyday life

Prep in words, then climb 2–3 prompts

You could talk about…
  • the technology you use most in an ordinary day
  • how it has changed the way you study
  • whether being contactable at all times is good or bad
  • a piece of technology you'd find hard to give up, and why
  • how everyday technology might look in twenty years
Listener's checklist: did they develop a prompt, or list all five? · notes, not a read-out speech? · did one idea lead into the next?
Clinic 3 · Developing an answer

Reason · Example · Contrast

The trap: the answer that stops after one sentence — "Yes, I like it." — and leaves the examiner to drag out more.
Reason
"…because it lets me…" — always ask yourself why.
Example
"…for instance, last week…" — a specific moment.
Contrast
"…though some people would say…" — the other view.
Run out of things to say? Reach for whichever engine you haven't used yet. There is always a reason, an example, or a contrast left.
Rep · one prompt, three engines · 45 seconds

Keep going: add a reason, then an example, then a contrast

Prompt: "Do you prefer studying with technology or with paper books?"
Start: "I prefer ______" → Reason "because ______"
→ Example "for instance, ______"
→ Contrast "although some people ______"
Listener: tick R · E · C as you hear each. A full answer uses all three from a single prompt — no new question needed.
Clinic 4 · When you run out

Hesitation that costs nothing

The trap
Filling every gap with "um… er…", or freezing into a long silence when a question surprises you — both read as a breakdown in fluency.
The fix · buy time in English
Use a fluent holding phrase while you think: "That's an interesting question…", "I've never really thought about it, but…", "Let me put it this way…". It sounds composed and buys you seconds.
A short phrase in fluent English is scored as fluency; a silence or "um" is not. Rehearse two until they're automatic.
Rep · open with a phrase, never a silence

B asks a surprise question; A buys time, then answers

Holding phrases (pick two)
"That's an interesting question…"
"I've never really thought about it, but…"
"Let me put it this way…"
"It depends, really — for example…"
Surprise questions
"Is it better to be busy or to have free time?"
"Should everyone learn to cook?"
"What would you change about your town?"
Listener: did A open with a phrase, not "um"? · no dead silence? · did the real answer then arrive?
Full paper · one take · A speaks, B is the examiner

Warm-up → prep → card → discussion

Card · "Money and young people"
how young people get money · a part-time job while studying? · something you saved for · how you decide if something's worth it · should schools teach managing money?
Then discuss
Should parents give a fixed weekly amount? · Save carefully, or enjoy what you have now? · Do people your age worry about money more than their parents did?
Leave your pauses in. Sitting the whole paper once — warts and all — teaches more than three tidy fragments, because what you're really rehearsing is recovering when a question catches you off guard.
Examiner (B): keep time, ask the discussion questions, and note one strength + one fix across all four skills. Then swap.

Five moves for the Speaking paper

1Use the warm-up to settle and find your voice — it sets your pace.
2Treat card prompts as a ladder — climb two or three, deeply.
3Develop every answer with Reason, Example, Contrast.
4Buy time with a fluent phrase — never "um" or silence.
5Rehearse the whole paper in one take — practise recovering, not reciting.
