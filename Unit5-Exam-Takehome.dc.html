Way in — before you read

When a picture made by an AI was hung in a real gallery, it forced everyone around it — curators, teachers, working artists — to decide what they actually believed. This article gathers how each group is responding, and why none of the answers is simple.

As you read, keep one question in mind: the text describes four different responses to AI art — can you name all four, and say which one the writer seems to find most sensible?

The text — annotate in the margin

Paragraphs are numbered for the exam. Use the right-hand margin for your Meaning · Method · Response notes.

1  When a small city museum quietly hung an AI-generated image among its paintings last spring, it did not expect a fight. Within a week the picture had drawn more visitors — and more arguments — than any work the gallery had shown in years. The image had simply made a question unavoidable: if a machine made it, does it belong on the wall at all? Around the country, curators, teachers and artists are now answering that question in very different ways.
Your notes ↓
2  The first response is refusal. A number of galleries and competitions have banned AI work outright, arguing that art must come from lived human experience. To these purists, an image assembled from millions of scraped pictures is not a creation but a very clever average. Their position is clear and easy to explain, though critics point out that photography and even ready-made objects were once refused on almost identical grounds.
Your notes ↓
3  A second, opposite response is to embrace the tools completely. Some artists now treat an AI model the way earlier generations treated the camera or the printing press: as one more instrument in the studio. They spend hours refining a single prompt, discarding hundreds of results, editing the survivors by hand. For them the machine removes drudgery, not creativity — and the interesting work, they insist, still happens in the choices a human makes.
Your notes ↓
4  The third response, and the one now spreading fastest, is neither ban nor open door but disclosure. Under this approach an AI-assisted work may be shown or entered for a prize, but its provenance must be declared: which tools were used, and where the training pictures came from. A label, supporters argue, lets the public judge honestly while protecting the artists whose work fed the machine. It is less a verdict than a safeguard.
Your notes ↓
5  Schools face a smaller version of the same choice, and most are landing in the same place. A growing number of art teachers now let students use AI tools for early ideas, on one firm condition: that they show their working and can explain every decision. The aim is not to catch cheats but to keep the thinking where it belongs — with the student. A picture no one can account for, they say, teaches nothing, however striking it looks.
Your notes ↓
6  None of this settles the deeper questions of consent and payment that still hang over the training data. But a pattern is emerging from the noise. The extremes — total ban, total welcome — are giving way to a quieter middle position built on honesty about where a picture comes from. It is not the most dramatic answer, and it will not satisfy everyone. It may, however, be the only one that lets the machine into the room without letting it decide what art is worth.
