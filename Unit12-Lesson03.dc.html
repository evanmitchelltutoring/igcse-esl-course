The Long Walk Out

Mark each decision

Maya had been walking for six hours when she finally admitted to herself that she was lost.

The trail had seemed obvious that morning — a clear line of stones winding up through the pines. But somewhere after the river crossing the path had faded, and the thick mist that rolled down from the peaks had swallowed every landmark she might have used. Now the light was failing, the temperature was dropping fast, and her water bottle was almost empty.

She made herself stop and think. Panicking, she knew, would only waste energy she could not spare. She remembered the one piece of advice her uncle, an experienced climber, had drummed into her before the trip: if you get lost, do not wander; find water and follow it downhill, because rivers lead to people. It was the only plan she had.

She found a thin stream within the hour and began to follow it, stumbling over wet rocks in the half-dark. Twice she fell. Her ankle, already sore, began to throb with every step, and there were moments when she wanted nothing more than to sit down in the cold and rest. She did not let herself. If she stopped now, she thought, she might never get up again.

The stream grew slowly into a noisy little river, and the river, at last, led her to a rough track. Maya could have wept with relief. She turned downhill and followed it until, rounding a bend, she saw the yellow squares of a farmhouse window glowing through the trees. The farmer who opened the door took one look at her — soaked, shaking, white with exhaustion — and pulled her inside without a word.

Later, wrapped in a blanket with a hot drink in her hands, Maya thought about how close it had been. If she had panicked, if she had sat down to rest, if she had not remembered her uncle's single rule, the night might have ended very differently. She had not done anything heroic, she decided. She had simply kept calm, kept moving, and trusted the water to do the rest.
