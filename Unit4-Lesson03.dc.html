Forty-Eight Hours in a City I Never Met

Read · then read again

Day one. We landed at noon and the list began. The old bridge — photo. The famous tower — photo, then a queue, then another photo from a better angle. By three o'clock my phone held sixty pictures and my feet ached. The guidebook called it 'the must-see heart of the city', and we saw it, the way you see a painting through a crowd: in glimpses, over shoulders.

Day two ran to the same rhythm. We queued two hours for the cathedral and twenty minutes for a lift to a rooftop viewpoint, where the city looked like its own postcard. A river cruise we had booked was cancelled — rain again — so we walked the old market instead, the one street I would happily have spent the whole trip in. By evening my legs had given up long before the list had.

What I remember now is not the tower. It is the small café where we stopped only because it rained. The owner, who spoke no English, drew a map on a napkin to the bus we had missed. For ten minutes we were not tourists ticking off a list; we were just people being helped by a stranger. Then the rain stopped, the list resumed, and we hurried on.

That night I scrolled through the day's photographs. They were good photographs. They proved I had been there. What they could not show was the ten minutes that actually mattered, because nobody photographs a stranger drawing a map. I kept scrolling anyway, as if the right picture might suddenly contain the thing I had missed.

I have travelled to nine countries and photographed all of them. If you asked me what any of them is truly like, I would struggle to answer. A tourist sees the surface and calls it the place. I am beginning to think the place is somewhere underneath, in the parts no itinerary makes time for.
