The Man Who Grows Forests on Rooftops

Reading · multiple choice

When Daniel Owusu tells people he is a farmer, they picture open fields. In fact his farms sit on the flat roofs of office blocks and car parks in the centre of one of the busiest cities in West Africa.

Owusu trained as an architect, not a gardener. For years he designed glass towers like everyone else, until a long illness forced him to spend months indoors looking out over a grey sea of concrete. 'I realised I had helped build a city with almost nowhere for anything green to live,' he says. 'I had been part of the problem.'

His idea was simple but stubborn: every unused roof, he argued, was a field waiting to happen. Persuading building owners was harder. Many feared the weight of the soil, or leaks, or the cost. Owusu's answer was to start small and let the results speak. He covered one modest rooftop with lightweight beds of vegetables and herbs, tended by people from the neighbourhood, and invited sceptical landlords to come and see.

The rooftop did more than grow food. In summer the planted roof kept the floor below it noticeably cooler, cutting the building's air-conditioning bills. Rainwater that had once rushed straight into overloaded drains was now soaked up by the soil. Bees and birds, almost unknown in the district, began to return. What had looked like charity turned out to make hard financial sense.

Today more than forty roofs are under cultivation, and a waiting list of owners want to join. Owusu is careful not to promise too much. 'A few gardens will not undo what cities have done to the natural world,' he warns. 'But they change how people think. Once someone has grown a tomato above the traffic, they never look at a bare roof the same way again. That shift in the mind is the real harvest.'

Skill · choosing the best answer

Read all four — then reject the traps

Rule 1 · Find the support
The right answer is the one the text actually says or clearly implies — find the line that proves it.
Rule 2 · Beware the trap
A wrong option often uses words from the text but changes the meaning. Match the idea, not just the words.

Grammar · talking about change

The present perfect — past reaching into now

have / has + past participle
"Bees have returned." · "Cities have done damage."
Use it for a change or result that matters now — not a finished time in the past.
Put the verb in the present perfect
a) More than forty roofs ____ (become) gardens.
b) Bees and birds ____ (return) to the district.
c) Owusu ____ (change) how people think.
Answers: a) have become · b) have returned · c) has changed
Fast finisher → write two present-perfect sentences about your town.
