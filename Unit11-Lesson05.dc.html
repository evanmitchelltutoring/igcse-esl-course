Exam skill · before we read

A fact can be checked. A claim may not.

The test that cuts through a hoax: could someone else check this? A fact survives the question; a claim leans only on who is speaking.
A fact
"The photo was taken on 3 May." — a date, a place, a measurement anyone could verify.
A claim
"It was definitely a monster." — an assertion that rests on the speaker, not the evidence.

Hoax or Real?

Number the three tests

Every few years, a photograph appears that seems to show something impossible: a strange creature in a lake, a light in the sky, a figure that should not be there. The pictures spread quickly, and for a while everyone argues. Then, often, the truth comes out, and it is rarely as exciting as the photograph.

So how can you tell a hoax from something real? The first question is not whether the thing is amazing, but who is telling you, and how they know. A claim is only as strong as its source. A blurred photo posted by an unknown account, with no date and no location, is weak evidence, however striking it looks. A clear report from several independent witnesses, who do not know each other and have nothing to gain, is far stronger.

The second test is whether the claim can be checked. Real evidence can be examined by other people: a photograph can be studied for signs of editing, a footprint can be measured. A hoax usually cannot survive this. Take the famous lake-monster photograph of the last century. Many years later, one of the men involved confessed: the monster had been a toy fixed to a floating wooden base and photographed close up. The picture had never been what it seemed.

A third warning sign is the story that fits a little too neatly. Hoaxers know what people want to see, and they give it to them. When a claim arrives perfectly formed, with no loose ends, a careful thinker becomes more suspicious, not less. Real events are usually messy; they leave questions behind.

None of this means you should believe nothing. Some genuine discoveries did, at first, seem impossible. The point is not to switch off your curiosity, but to switch on your judgement.

Answer · mark your own

Fact or claim?

1. F — a detail anyone could check.
2. C — "obviously" rests on the speaker, not evidence.
3. F — shown, testable, and confessed.
4. F — independent accounts can be compared.
5. C — "anyone can see… proves" claims far more than it shows.

Red-flag words: obviously, definitely, anyone can see, it proves — a claim dressed as a fact.
