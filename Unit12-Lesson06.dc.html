A Night on the Mountain

Mark mistakes & the good call

I have been asked to describe, as clearly as I can, what happened on the mountain that night, while I still remember it well.

We had set out late — that was our first mistake. By the time we reached the ridge, the afternoon light was already fading, and a cold wind had begun to rise from the valley below. I suggested turning back, but the others wanted to push on to the summit, and I did not argue as firmly as I should have.

The mist came down without warning. One moment we could see the path ahead; the next, we could see almost nothing. We lost the trail completely. Rather than wander on in the dark, we did the one sensible thing we did all day: we stopped, found a hollow that was sheltered from the wind, and waited for morning. We had no tent, but we had a little food and, luckily, a survival blanket, which we shared between us.

It was a long, cold night. To stay awake and keep our spirits up, we took turns to talk. Nobody slept much. I will be honest: there was a point, in the small hours, when I was genuinely frightened that one of us might not make it to morning.

At first light the mist lifted, and we saw, to our astonishment, that we had stopped barely two hundred metres from a marked path. We walked down slowly and were met by a mountain rescue team who had been about to come looking for us. I am told we were very lucky. I believe it.

Sequence & honesty

1. They set out late.
2. That they should have turned back — "I did not argue as firmly as I should have".
3. They stopped, sheltered in a hollow, and waited rather than wandering on.
4. They took turns to talk and shared a survival blanket.
5. They had stopped barely 200 metres from a marked path.

Owning the mistakes is what makes the recount honest — and believable.
