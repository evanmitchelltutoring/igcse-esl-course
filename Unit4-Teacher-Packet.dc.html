The reading texts
Forty-Eight Hours in a City I Never Met
Reading text

1Day one. We landed at noon and the list began. The old bridge — photo. The famous tower — photo, then a queue, then another photo from a better angle. By three o'clock my phone held sixty pictures and my feet ached. The guidebook called it 'the must-see heart of the city', and we saw it, the way you see a painting through a crowd: in glimpses, over shoulders.

2Day two ran to the same rhythm. We queued two hours for the cathedral and twenty minutes for a lift to a rooftop viewpoint, where the city looked like its own postcard. A river cruise we had booked was cancelled — rain again — so we walked the old market instead, the one street I would happily have spent the whole trip in. By evening my legs had given up long before the list had.

3What I remember now is not the tower. It is the small café where we stopped only because it rained. The owner, who spoke no English, drew a map on a napkin to the bus we had missed. For ten minutes we were not tourists ticking off a list; we were just people being helped by a stranger. Then the rain stopped, the list resumed, and we hurried on.

4That night I scrolled through the day's photographs. They were good photographs. They proved I had been there. What they could not show was the ten minutes that actually mattered, because nobody photographs a stranger drawing a map. I kept scrolling anyway, as if the right picture might suddenly contain the thing I had missed.

5I have travelled to nine countries and photographed all of them. If you asked me what any of them is truly like, I would struggle to answer. A tourist sees the surface and calls it the place. I am beginning to think the place is somewhere underneath, in the parts no itinerary makes time for.

The Month I Stopped Translating
Reading text

1For the first weeks, everything was a small battle. The washing machine had symbols I did not understand. The bread was the wrong shape. I translated every sentence in my head before I dared to speak it, and by the time the words came out, the moment had usually passed. I told my family on the phone that I was fine. I was not sure it was true.

2The strangeness lived in small things. The light switched off downstairs, not up. Evenings arrived an hour earlier than my body expected, so that dusk always felt like an accusation that I had wasted the day. I carried a phrase book the way other people carry an umbrella — not often opened, but never left behind.

3Then, somewhere in the second month, something quietly shifted. I bought bread without rehearsing the question. I knew which neighbour smiled back and which did not. One evening I caught myself laughing at a joke before I had finished translating it — and realised I had not translated it at all. The change had no edges. It was less an arrival than a tide that had come in while I was looking the other way.

4Nobody arrived to tell me I belonged. There was no certificate, no welcome. There was only a Tuesday that felt, for no particular reason, ordinary. I walked the same street I had once found bewildering, and it was simply the way home.

5I still miss things I cannot always name. But I have learned that belonging is not a door you walk through. It arrives in pieces, on days you are not watching for it, until one morning the unfamiliar has quietly become yours.

Two Languages, One Self
Reading text

1Interviewer: You grew up speaking two languages. Does one feel more 'you' than the other?

2Lina: People expect me to choose, but I cannot. In one language I am louder, quicker to joke. In the other I am gentler — it is the language I say sorry in. They are both me. I am just a slightly different me in each.

3Interviewer: Do you switch without noticing?

4Lina: All the time. At home, a sentence might start in one language and finish in the other. My grandmother calls it 'making a mess'. I call it normal. The strange part is doing it instinctively — my mouth chooses before my mind does.

5Interviewer: Has it ever caused trouble?

6Lina: Small things. A word that is polite in one language sounds rude in the other if you translate it directly. I have apologised for jokes that were never insults. But honestly, the harder part is not the words. It is that some people think you must belong fully to one side or the other. I have spent years learning that the in-between is a real place to stand — not a failure to land.

7Interviewer: Is there anything people misunderstand about growing up between two cultures?

8Lina: That it must be confusing all the time. Mostly it is not — it is just ordinary to me. What is hard is the assumption that I am half of two things instead of fully something new. I am not fifty per cent one and fifty per cent another. I am one hundred per cent in-between, and that turned out to be its own whole thing.

9Interviewer: What would you tell a younger version of yourself?

10Lina: That the discomfort fades. For years I tried to pick a side to make other people comfortable. Now I let the two languages sit in the same sentence and I do not apologise for it. The mess, it turns out, was never a mess. It was just me.

Coming Home Changed
Reading text

1Most travellers prepare for culture shock — the disorientation of arriving somewhere new. Far fewer expect its mirror image: reverse culture shock, the strange difficulty of returning home.

2Researchers describe it in stages. At first, returnees feel relief and excitement at familiar faces and food. This 'honeymoon' fades within weeks. A second stage often brings frustration: home has carried on without them, friends have moved on, and stories from abroad are met with polite but brief interest. Returnees may feel that they have changed while everything they left behind has stayed the same — or worse, that it has changed in ways they no longer recognise. The feeling can be disorienting precisely because it is unexpected: the traveller braced for a foreign country, not for a strange version of their own.

3A third stage is gradual readjustment. Returnees learn to hold two things at once: the person they were before and the person they became away. Many report that they never fully return to who they were. They notice things about home they once took for granted, and they carry habits, tastes and questions picked up elsewhere.

4Psychologists who study returnees suggest several ways to ease the transition. Keeping in contact with friends made abroad helps the experience feel real rather than imagined. Joining a returnee group or an online community reduces the sense of being alone with a story no one wants to hear. And keeping one or two habits from the host country — a food, a phrase, a routine — lets the person they were abroad stay alive in the person they are at home. None of these removes the strangeness overnight, but each gives the returning traveller something to hold while the familiar slowly fits again.

5The length of the adjustment varies from person to person. As a rough guide, researchers note that the deeper the immersion and the longer the time away, the longer the readjustment tends to take. Knowing this in advance does not remove the discomfort, but it makes it easier to recognise and to wait out.

6Experts suggest that the experience, though uncomfortable, is valuable. The discomfort is evidence of growth. A person who feels nothing on returning has usually engaged with very little; the ache is simply the price of having been changed. What stays with a traveller is rarely the photographs. More often it is a wider sense of how many ordinary ways there are to live a life.

9b · "Watch for" — misconceptions specific to this unit
Present perfect (L7)Base verb for participle ("have visit"); since + a length ("since two years"); adverb misplaced. Drill for + length vs since + start, and ever/never/just/already between have and the participle.
Relative clauses (L8)Keeping the repeated word ("which it", "where… there") and who/which/where mismatch. Drill: people → who, things → which, places → where; drop the repeat.
Reading for feeling (L4)Stopping at "it's sad". Require name-it-then-prove-it: the feeling AND the word/image that carries it.
Short answers (L3)Over-writing. In Part 1, extra wrong detail can cancel a correct answer — answer, then delete what wasn't asked.
Description (L11)Telling with adjectives ("it was nice/old") instead of showing with sensory detail. Insist on things the reader can see, hear or smell.
Register (L10–11)Article/description drifting into a bare list of events. Require reflection (Resonance) and a verdict — what the place taught or made of the writer.
9c · Support / Stretch
Support — give the frames: "I have ___ since ___." · "…is a place where ___." · "What surprised me was ___." Word bank: settle in · unfamiliar · gradually · nostalgia · belonging · perspective · adapt. Provide the article/description skeleton pre-labelled so students only fill content.
Stretch — combine present perfect and a relative clause in one sentence ("This is the street where I have lived for ten years"); replace every "told" adjective with a "shown" detail; deliver a 60-second spoken version of the description.

Travel Moments: Eight Short Recordings
Answers
1 B — 14:40  ·  2 C — Take your shoes off at the door  ·  3 C — Being understood when joking  ·  4 B — Inside the temple  ·  5 C — Frustrated that people want a simple answer  ·  6 A — The library  ·  7 A — She has settled in unusually fast  ·  8 A — It turned out to be worthwhile
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

[Eight short recordings. TTS production, mixed accents, ~135 wpm.]

1. (Airport announcement) Passengers on the delayed flight to Lisbon: your new departure time is 14:40, not 14:14 as shown earlier. Please go to gate 22.

2. (Homestay host) We eat dinner at seven, but help yourself to fruit any time. The only rule is shoes off at the door.

3. (Student) I thought I would miss the food most. Actually, what I miss is being able to make a joke and have everyone understand it.

4. (Tour guide) Photos are fine outside, but inside the temple please put your cameras away. It is a place of worship, not a backdrop.

5. (Returnee) Everyone asked how my trip was, and they wanted a one-line answer. How do you fit a whole year into one line?

6. (Announcement) The language exchange now meets on Thursdays at six, in the library, not the café. The café was too noisy last term.

7. (Host parent) She has only been here three weeks and she already orders her own bread at the bakery. That took our last guest months.

8. (Traveller) The map said twenty minutes. Two hours and three wrong turns later I finally found the hostel — and honestly, I saw more of the city that way.

Why I Travelled: Five Short Extracts
Answers
1 A — challenge themselves  ·  2 B — tourism is harming the place they love  ·  3 B — living with a family teaches more than touring  ·  4 C — nervous at first, then very glad  ·  5 B — giving advice
Transcript — read twice, pausing between items

[Five short extracts. TTS production.]

Speaker 1: I do not travel to relax. A beach bores me in a day. I go to put myself somewhere I do not know the rules and see how I cope.

Speaker 2: I love that town, which is exactly why it hurts to see it now — souvenir shops where the bakery used to be, queues where the quiet square once was.

Speaker 3: A hotel keeps you comfortable and separate. Living with a family for a month, eating what they eat, even arguing about the washing-up — that taught me more than any tour.

Speaker 4: I almost did not go. I was so nervous at the airport I nearly turned around. I am so glad I did not; it became the best year of my life.

Speaker 5: My one tip? Learn ten words before you land — hello, please, thank you, sorry, and the numbers one to five. People forgive everything if you try.
