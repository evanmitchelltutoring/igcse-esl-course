The Homework Gap

Mark the figures & the fixes

Every evening, two students sit down to do the same homework. One opens a laptop in a quiet room and connects to fast, reliable internet. The other shares a single phone with younger siblings, hunts for a signal near the window, and waits for pages to load. They are set the same task and judged by the same standard. They do not start from the same place.

Teachers have a name for this: the homework gap. It describes the difference in learning that comes not from ability or effort, but from what students can access at home. A recent school survey found that nearly one in three students did not have a quiet place to study, and roughly one in five had no dependable internet connection.

The gap is easy to miss because it is invisible in the classroom. A student who hands in unfinished work may look careless, when in truth they ran out of data at nine o'clock the night before. Marks fall, confidence follows, and a problem that began with resources slowly turns into a belief that the student is 'just not good at school'.

Some schools have started to respond. A few now lend laptops and portable internet devices to students who need them. Others keep the library open late so that anyone can find a quiet desk and a steady connection. One head teacher described the change simply: when the tools were shared more equally, the marks of the lowest-achieving group rose within a single term.

None of these measures removes inequality entirely. A borrowed laptop does not buy a quiet home. But they show something important — that part of what looks like a gap in talent is really a gap in access, and that access is something schools and communities can choose to change.
