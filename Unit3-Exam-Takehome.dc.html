● Response
How do you react? Note a question, a surprise, or what the writer wants you to feel.
Way in — before you read

Many young athletes believe that the harder they push, the better they will do — and that resting is a sign of weakness. This text follows one talented footballer who believed exactly that, until it nearly cost him the sport he loved.

As you read, keep one question in mind: does the writer think Jordan's old belief — "more is always better" — was right or wrong, and how can you tell?

The text — annotate in the margin

Paragraphs are numbered for the exam. Use the right-hand margin for your Meaning · Method · Response notes.

1  At fifteen, Jordan Achebe was the player every coach wanted: the first to arrive at training, the last to leave, the one who ran extra laps when everyone else had gone home. By seventeen, he had almost given up the sport entirely. His story has since become a quiet warning passed around changing rooms across the country.
Your notes ↓
2  Jordan's problem was not laziness; it was the opposite. He had absorbed a single, dangerous belief — that more must always be better. Tired legs, he told himself, were a sign of weakness rather than a signal from his body. So he trained through aches that should have been rested, and treated every defeat as proof that he simply had not worked hard enough.
Your notes ↓
3  The breaking point came not on the pitch but in a classroom, when he could no longer concentrate and felt strangely flat about a sport he had once loved. A team doctor used a word Jordan had never applied to himself: burnout. 'I thought rest was something you earned,' he says now. 'Nobody had told me it was something you needed.'
Your notes ↓
4  What followed was slower and less dramatic than any training session. Jordan learned to read his own tiredness, to take recovery days without guilt, and to measure progress over months rather than hours. His performance did not collapse, as he had feared. Within a year it had quietly improved.
Your notes ↓
5  Perhaps the biggest change was in the kind of teammate he became. The boy who once judged others for resting now noticed when a friend was struggling and told them, gently, to ease off. When he was made captain last season, his coach pointed to exactly this. 'Jordan leads by permission,' she explained. 'He gives the younger players permission to look after themselves — and that makes the whole team stronger.'
Your notes ↓
6  Jordan is careful not to turn his story into a lecture. He still trains hard; he is still ambitious. But he no longer wears exhaustion like a medal. The lesson he offers younger players is short, and he repeats it often: your body is not the enemy. Learn to listen to it.
