The reading texts
What Counts as a Sport?
Reading text

1When officials decide whether an activity deserves to be called a sport, they rarely rely on instinct. Most governing bodies weigh four broad criteria: physical exertion, skill, organised competition, and a recognised governing body. An activity that satisfies all four has a strong claim; one that misses several does not.

2Esports — competitive video gaming played for prizes in front of an audience — is often measured against exactly these tests, and the results are revealing.

3Take organised competition. Here esports stands on firm ground. Professional titles run structured leagues, seasons, play-offs and world championships, with rules enforced by referees and anti-cheating systems as strict as any in traditional sport.

4Governing bodies, too, increasingly exist. National esports federations have formed in dozens of countries, and international associations now represent the sport's interests, much as football or athletics organisations do.

5Skill is rarely disputed. Top players train for hours every day to sharpen decision-making, teamwork and precision under pressure. Few who have watched a high-level match would call it easy.

6The one criterion that remains contested is physical exertion. Critics argue that sitting at a screen, however intense the concentration, cannot compare to the bodily effort of running or swimming. Supporters reply that exertion comes in many forms — the rapid, repeated hand movements and split-second reactions of elite players are physical, even if they do not leave a player breathless.

7This is the heart of the debate. If physical exertion is treated as the single decisive test, esports struggles. If it is one factor among four, esports scores well on the rest.

8For now, the question stays open. Some bodies have embraced esports; others hold back, unconvinced that a contest decided by fingertips belongs in the same category as one decided by whole bodies. What is clear is that the four criteria, not personal taste, are where the argument is fought.

The Business Behind the Team
Reading text

1A successful esports organisation looks less like a group of friends gaming and more like a small sports business. Behind the five players on stage sits a structure built to find talent, fund it and keep it winning.

2Money arrives from several directions. The largest share for most teams is sponsorship — brands pay to place their logos on jerseys and streams. A second source is prize money, won at tournaments and shared between the organisation and its players. A third, fast-growing source is streaming revenue, earned when players broadcast their practice to online audiences who subscribe or donate. Ticket sales from live events add a smaller amount, and selling merchandise such as jerseys and hoodies brings in the rest.

3The people involved go far beyond the players. Each team employs a head coach, who designs strategy and reviews matches. An analyst studies opponents and prepares data on their habits. A team manager handles travel, contracts and daily life, while many organisations now add a sports psychologist to protect players' focus and confidence. Larger teams also run an academy — a junior squad where promising young players develop before joining the main roster.

4Yet the career is far from secure. Professional play is unusually short: most competitors retire in their mid-twenties, when reaction speeds begin to slow. Rosters change constantly, and a player who underperforms for a few weeks may be dropped. Few earn the headline salaries reported in the news; for every star, many more get by on modest pay and uncertain contracts. Burnout, caused by long practice hours and relentless pressure, ends careers early.

5This mix of real money and real risk is part of why supporters insist esports should be taken seriously. The structures — salaries, coaching staff, academies, enforced rules — mirror those of any professional sport. Critics counter that money alone proves nothing: plenty of activities pay well without being called sports. Still, the professional machinery surrounding top teams is difficult to dismiss as mere play.

Bodies Pushed Hard
Reading text

1Ask whether professional gamers are athletes and you will start an argument. Look closely at what their bodies actually do, however, and the question becomes harder to dismiss.

2The clearest physical demand is speed. Top players register reaction times far faster than the average adult, responding to events on screen in fractions of a second. In some games they perform several hundred deliberate actions every minute — a rate of precise hand and finger movement that few activities require. Holding that accuracy for hours, match after match, calls for fine motor control and intense concentration rather than brute strength.

3This precision comes at a cost. Years of rapid, repeated movements can cause repetitive strain injuries in the wrists and hands, ending careers as surely as a torn muscle ends a runner's. Long hours seated bring back and neck problems, and constant focus on a bright screen tires the eyes. The body is not driven to exhaustion the way a sprinter's is, but it is pushed hard in its own narrow way.

4Teams have responded much as traditional clubs do. Many now employ sports scientists to measure performance and recovery. Physiotherapists treat and prevent the strain injuries that threaten careers. Nutritionists plan meals to keep energy and focus steady through long sessions, and some organisations enforce fitness routines, arguing that a fitter body supports a sharper mind. Sleep, once ignored, is now managed carefully.

5Whether all this makes a gamer an athlete depends on what the word means. If an athlete is someone whose body must be trained, protected and pushed to compete at the highest level, professional players have a case. If the word demands large-scale physical exertion — the heaving lungs of a swimmer or cyclist — then the case is weaker.

6What is not in doubt is that the body matters. The teams paying for physiotherapists and sports scientists are not doing it for show; they do it because, at the top level, the gap between winning and losing can come down to how well a player's hands, eyes and mind hold up under pressure. The argument over the word "athlete" continues, but the physical reality behind it is no longer easy to wave away.

The Roaring Dark
Reading text

1The arena is dark, and then it isn't. Eighteen thousand phone lights bloom at once, a galaxy held in human hands, and the noise that follows hits the chest like a physical thing. On stage, five players sit in a pool of light, hunched over keyboards, utterly still while everything around them erupts. They are about to play a video game. The crowd is about to lose its mind.

2If you still picture esports as a hobby for lonely teenagers in darkened bedrooms, this is the scene that should give you pause.

3Tickets to events like this sell out in minutes. The biggest finals fill stadiums built for football and basketball, and for every fan inside, hundreds of thousands more are watching at home. A single championship can draw millions of concurrent viewers — more, on some nights, than the traditional sports finals shown at the same hour. The audience is not imaginary. It is measurable, vast and growing.

4And it behaves exactly as sports crowds always have. People paint their faces in team colours. They gasp as one, groan as one, surge to their feet as one. They queue for merchandise and they weep when their heroes lose. Strip away the screens and the controllers, and what remains is the oldest spectacle in the world: thousands of strangers bound together for a few hours by the fortunes of a team they have chosen to love.

5So here is the question the doubters must answer. If a contest fills arenas, sells out broadcasts and moves a crowd to tears, on what grounds is it not a spectator sport? The audience has already decided. The only people still arguing, it sometimes seems, are the ones who have never sat in that roaring dark and felt the floor shake.

6None of this proves that esports demands the body of an Olympic sprinter. But spectacle was never only about the athletes. It was always, also, about us — the watchers, the believers, the ones who show up. And by that measure, esports arrived a long time ago.

5 · Grammar (Use of English)

5a · Second (unreal) conditional

If + past simple, would + base form. No would in the if-clause; use were for all persons.

  1. Fix: "If I would train harder, I would win."
  2. Make a second conditional: (we / have / a coach → we / train / better)
  3. Complete: "If our striker ___ (be) fitter, he ___ (score) more goals." __________
5b · Relative clauses (who / which / that / where)
  1. Join: "A champion is a person. This person never gives up."
  2. Add a non-defining clause: "The club ___ drew strong interest."

6 · Writing

6a · A directed report — "Esports club survey" (150–200 words)
Shape: Introduction (purpose + source) → Findings (headings: interest / benefits / concerns) → Analysis + recommendation.   Impersonal register (no "I think"); a non-defining relative clause; target words (criterion · legitimate · governing body · recognition).
6b · An argumentative essay — "Esports is a real sport." How far do you agree? (120–160 words)
Shape: Stance → Concession (grant the best objection) → Reasoning (evidence: the four tests) → Your strongest point → Verdict.   Use a concession chunk and 4+ argument chunks/target words (on balance · the evidence suggests · there is a strong case that · while critics argue · criterion · exertion). Land a verdict — don't just repeat the question.
