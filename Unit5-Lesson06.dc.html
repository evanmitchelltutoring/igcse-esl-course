Should a Machine Win an Art Prize?

Read · then read again

In 2022, a picture made with an AI program won first place in a digital-art competition in the United States. The artist had typed prompts, chosen from hundreds of results, and refined the final image — but no brush, pen, or camera was involved. The win caused an immediate controversy, and the questions it raised have not gone away.

The case for accepting AI art. Supporters say the result should be judged on its artistic merit alone: if a picture moves people, it should not matter how it was made. They point to history — photography was once dismissed as mere button-pressing, yet now hangs in great galleries. AI art, they argue, is simply the newest medium. And humans are still involved — choosing prompts, selecting outputs, editing — so it is not purely automatic. To them it is a legitimate form of expression that deserves recognition.

The case against. Opponents' central objection is fairness: if an AI was trained on human artists' work who were never paid, any prize it wins is built on borrowed labour. Allowing it to compete is unfair to artists who spent years developing their skill. There is also a worry about value: if perfect-looking images can be made in seconds by anyone, the special status of art — and artists' income — may be undermined. Some fear it would damage the credibility of the prizes themselves.

The middle ground. Many take a more cautious view. They do not want to ban AI art, but they want transparency: entries should at least be labelled, so judges and viewers know what they are looking at. Several competitions have responded by creating a separate AI category — keeping the contest fair while still making room for the new tool.
