The Vanishing Village

Circle the clues

In 1930, a supply ship arrived at a remote island off the coast of Scotland to relieve the men who kept watch over its small settlement of sheep farmers and their families. The crew expected the usual welcome: smoke rising from chimneys, dogs barking, someone waving from the jetty. Instead, they found nothing. The village of nineteen houses stood silent in the mist.

The crew went ashore and searched from house to house. Fires had been laid in several grates but never lit. A half-eaten meal sat on a table in one cottage, the chair pushed back as though someone had risen in a hurry. In another house, a Bible lay open on a windowsill, a knife resting across its pages. Clothes hung on lines outside, still damp. There was no sign of struggle anywhere, and no sign of the thirty-six villagers who had lived there only weeks before.

The last letter sent from the island, found half-written at a desk, mentioned nothing unusual — only a note about the weather turning and a plan to mend a boat before winter. The islanders' small fishing boats were still tied up at the jetty, which ruled out the simplest explanation: that they had left together by sea.

Over the decades, theories multiplied. Some said a sudden storm drove the villagers to a neighbouring island, though no record of their arrival was ever found. Others suggested a disease frightened them into fleeing overland, although the cliffs made such a journey almost impossible on foot.

What is agreed is this: the meal was left unfinished, the fires unlit, and the Bible marked at a page no one would ever read again. To this day, no bone, no boat, and no letter has ever explained what became of the people of that island.
