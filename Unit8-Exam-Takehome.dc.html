Way in — before you read

We talk about "going to Mars" as if it were a long flight. It isn't. The planet is a moving target hundreds of millions of kilometres away, and the journey tests fuel, the human body and the human mind to their limits. This text sets out the three great challenges — and why scientists still believe it is a question of when, not if.

Predict: which of the three challenges do you think is hardest to solve — and why?

The text — annotate in the margin

Paragraphs are numbered for the exam. Use the right-hand margin for your Gist · Detail · Structure notes.

1  Sending humans to Mars is one of the great ambitions of our time, but it is far harder than most people imagine. The planet is, on average, about 225 million kilometres from Earth, and the two worlds are constantly moving. Engineers cannot simply aim at where Mars is today; they must aim at where it will be when the spacecraft arrives, roughly seven months later.
Your notes ↓
2  The first great challenge is fuel. A rocket large enough to escape Earth must carry an enormous amount of fuel, and most of that weight is burned in the first few minutes of flight. To save weight, engineers plan to send supplies ahead of the crew on separate, uncrewed flights. The astronauts would then arrive to find food and equipment already waiting on the surface.
Your notes ↓
3  The second challenge is radiation. In space, far from the protection of Earth's magnetic field, the crew would be exposed to harmful rays from the Sun and from deep space. Too much radiation can damage the body's cells and raise the risk of cancer. Designers are testing shields made of water and even of plastic, because surprisingly these light materials block radiation better than metal does.
Your notes ↓
4  The third challenge is the human mind. A round trip could last almost three years, and for much of that time the crew would be trapped together in a small space, millions of kilometres from home. Loneliness, boredom and arguments are real dangers. Space agencies now run long isolation experiments on Earth, sealing volunteers inside a fake spacecraft for months to learn how people cope.
Your notes ↓
5  Despite all this, scientists remain hopeful. Every problem, they argue, is one that careful engineering and patient testing can solve. The journey to Mars may take decades to prepare, but most experts believe it is a question of when, not if.

Before you come to the exam, check you can…
  • name the three challenges: fuel → radiation → the human mind;
  • give the key figures: 225 million km, seven months out, a three-year round trip;
  • explain why supplies are sent ahead, and why water and plastic beat metal as shields;
  • say what the writer believes overall — and point to the phrase that shows it ("when, not if").
