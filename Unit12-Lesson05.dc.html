Crossing the White

Underline feeling words

People ask me why anyone would choose to walk across a frozen wilderness, dragging everything they need behind them on a sledge. I am not sure I have ever given a good answer. The truth is that the ice gets into you, and you keep going back.

My third expedition was the hardest. For five weeks my partner and I hauled our sledges across a great white plain, hundreds of miles from the nearest human being. There is a particular kind of silence out there that I have found nowhere else — no birds, no traffic, no voices, only the hiss of the runners and the sound of your own breathing. Some people find it unbearable. I have come to love it.

The cold, of course, is the constant enemy. At those temperatures a moment of carelessness — a glove pulled off for too long, a zip left open — can cost you your fingers. Every task that would take seconds at home takes minutes, performed with stiff hands and enormous concentration. You learn to do everything slowly and to check everything twice, because out there a small mistake does not stay small.

What surprised me most, on that third crossing, was not the hardship but how the mind behaves. There were days when I felt I could walk forever, and days when putting up the tent felt beyond me. I learned that the body will usually do far more than the mind believes, if you can only keep the mind from giving up. On the worst afternoon, when a storm pinned us in the tent for thirty hours, my partner said something I have never forgotten: the secret, she said, was not to think about the whole journey, only about the next hour. Just get through the next hour, and then the next.

We reached the far coast on the thirty-eighth day, thinner, slower and quieter than we had set out. People imagine a great moment of triumph at the end of such a journey. In truth I mostly felt a deep tiredness, and a strange sadness that it was over. Within a year, of course, I was planning the next one. The ice, as I said, gets into you.

Attitude & language

1. She loves it — "I have come to love it" — though others find it "unbearable".
2. The extreme cold — stiff hands, and a small mistake "does not stay small".
3. Not the hardship but how the mind behaves — the body does more than the mind believes.
4. A "deep tiredness" and "strange sadness" — surprising because we expect triumph.
5. That the ice has an almost irresistible pull — she can't stay away.
