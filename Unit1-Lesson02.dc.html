The Café That Never Asks You to Leave

Reading · core

In Seoul, a "study café" looks nothing like an ordinary coffee shop. There are no sofas and no background chatter — just long desks, soft lamps, a power socket at every seat, and rows of students with headphones on. The idea spread quickly across Asia, and today similar cafés are common in Bangkok, where they fill up fastest during exam season.

The appeal is simple: space. Many students live in small flats with noisy younger siblings, and school libraries close early. A study café stays open late — some in Bangkok do not lock their doors until midnight — and charges by time rather than by drink. A typical rate is around 60 baht an hour, often with free refills of water, tea or weak coffee.

Lalita, 17, comes three or four times a week. "At home I get distracted within ten minutes," she says. "Here everyone is working, so I work too. It's quieter than the library, and nobody rushes you." That last point matters. In an ordinary café there is often an unwritten rule — one drink buys you about two hours before a waiter starts hovering. Study cafés remove that pressure completely.

For owners, the model is steady rather than spectacular. "Students don't spend much on each visit," explains one Bangkok café owner, "but they come back again and again, and they bring their friends." Roughly seven in ten of his customers are under twenty. He keeps the prices low and the wifi fast, and trusts that loyalty will do the rest.

Not everyone is convinced the trend is healthy. Some teachers worry that paying to study turns concentration into something only wealthier students can afford. Others point out that, for a teenager with nowhere quiet to go, 60 baht for a calm afternoon may be the cheapest, kindest deal in the city.
