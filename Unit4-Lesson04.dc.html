The Month I Stopped Translating

Read · then read again

For the first weeks, everything was a small battle. The washing machine had symbols I did not understand. The bread was the wrong shape. I translated every sentence in my head before I dared to speak it, and by the time the words came out, the moment had usually passed. I told my family on the phone that I was fine. I was not sure it was true.

The strangeness lived in small things. The light switched off downstairs, not up. Evenings arrived an hour earlier than my body expected, so that dusk always felt like an accusation that I had wasted the day. I carried a phrase book the way other people carry an umbrella — not often opened, but never left behind.

Then, somewhere in the second month, something quietly shifted. I bought bread without rehearsing the question. I knew which neighbour smiled back and which did not. One evening I caught myself laughing at a joke before I had finished translating it — and realised I had not translated it at all. The change had no edges. It was less an arrival than a tide that had come in while I was looking the other way.

Nobody arrived to tell me I belonged. There was no certificate, no welcome. There was only a Tuesday that felt, for no particular reason, ordinary. I walked the same street I had once found bewildering, and it was simply the way home.

I still miss things I cannot always name. But I have learned that belonging is not a door you walk through. It arrives in pieces, on days you are not watching for it, until one morning the unfamiliar has quietly become yours.
