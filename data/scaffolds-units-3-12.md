# Per-unit scaffolds — IGCSE ESL Units 3–12

Graphic organisers (pre-filled from each passage) + tiered **Support** / **Stretch**, matching the Units 1–2 template (§P2.4 tiered tasks · §P2.5 graphic organisers). For B1–B2 EAL learners. Organiser entries are drawn from the real text so a teacher can see the frame works before handing it out. Answer-style entries here are **teacher-facing models**.

Organiser types used: **cause → effect** (arrow boxes) · **for | against** (T-chart) · **compare** (double-bubble: A | both | B) · **sequence / timeline**.

## UNIT 3 · Health & Wellbeing

### "The Energy Trap"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *What one energy drink really does.*
  - caffeine + sugar (up to 2 coffees' worth in one can) → **a short, sharp lift**
  - the lift wears off after 1–2 hours → **a crash: more tired and hungrier than before**
  - students rely on them in exam season → **poorer sleep + trouble concentrating (the opposite of the promise)**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Energy drinks do not make energy; they ___ it, and the crash leaves you ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 3 (the paragraph beginning "The promised 'energy'…").
- **Stretch:** The writer says the drinks "borrow" energy. Why choose the word *borrow* rather than *give*? · The writer ends "real, lasting energy comes from sleep, food and movement — not from a can." How does ending on those three ordinary things change how you feel about the cans?

### "The Science of a Good Night's Sleep"
- **Organiser — sequence (stages through the night).** Title: *What the brain does while you sleep.*
  1. **Light sleep** — body relaxes, heartbeat slows.
  2. **Deep (slow-wave) sleep** — releases growth hormone; repairs muscle and bone.
  3. **REM sleep** — eyes flick; dreaming; the brain moves new learning into long-term memory.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"During deep sleep the body ___, and during REM sleep the brain ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3 (deep sleep, then REM).
- **Stretch:** The writer calls a bedroom screen "one of the worst enemies of rest." Why use the strong word *enemy* for a phone? · Which single word in the last paragraph tells you the cure is easy as well as effective?

### "The Captain Who Learned to Rest"
- **Organiser — sequence / timeline (a change over time).** Title: *Jordan's turning point.*
  - **At 15** — first to arrive, last to leave; believed "more must always be better."
  - **At 17** — burnout; could not concentrate, felt "flat" about the sport he loved.
  - **After** — learned to rest without guilt; performance quietly improved; became a captain who "leads by permission."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Before, Jordan thought rest was something you ___; now he knows it is something you ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 3 (the doctor's word "burnout").
- **Stretch:** Jordan "no longer wears exhaustion like a medal." What does this comparison show about how he used to feel about being tired? · The coach says he "leads by permission." Why is this a surprising kind of leadership?

### "The Two-Minute Workout"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *Why 'exercise snacks' work.*
  - each burst is only 1–2 minutes → **people actually do them, and feel less discouraged**
  - short bursts raise the heart rate → **heart fitness improves almost as much as one long workout**
  - "stack" a snack onto a habit you never skip (e.g. after brushing teeth) → **the movement becomes automatic**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"An exercise snack is a short burst of ___, repeated ___ across the day."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 for the definition.
- **Stretch:** The writer chooses the food word "snack" for exercise. Why is that a helpful comparison for a busy student? · What is the writer's attitude to the student who says "I have no time to exercise"?

### "Why We Yawn"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *Two ideas about yawning.*
  - a deep breath of air → **cools the brain slightly → helps us stay alert** (may explain yawning before a race)
  - seeing/hearing/reading about a yawn → **we "catch" it** — more easily from someone we feel close to
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"One theory is that a yawn helps to ___ the brain, so that we feel more ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 for the "cooling" idea.
- **Stretch:** The writer says "you may well feel the urge right now." Why speak directly to the reader here? · The text calls yawning "one of those small, everyday mysteries." What does this phrase suggest about how much science really knows?

## UNIT 4 · Living Abroad, Travel & Origins

### "Becoming a Local: How People Settle into a New Country"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes), one row per factor.** Title: *What helps a newcomer feel at home.*
  - build small daily habits (same route, same shop, same café) → **daily life feels manageable, not a test**
  - learn even a little of the language → **feel far less isolated**
  - make one or two local friends → **learn the "unwritten rules" no guidebook contains**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"People who settle well build small daily ___, and they treat differences as ___ rather than wrong."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 5 (routine; attitude).
- **Stretch:** The writer ends "arrival… is less an event than a habit you practise until it is true." Why compare belonging to a habit? · The passage says the people who settle best "are rarely the luckiest." What quality does the writer value more than luck?

### "Coming Home Changed: The Shock Nobody Warns You About"
- **Organiser — sequence (stages of reverse culture shock).** Title: *Coming home, stage by stage.*
  1. **Honeymoon** — relief and excitement at familiar faces and food (fades within weeks).
  2. **Frustration** — home has moved on; stories from abroad get "polite but brief interest."
  3. **Readjustment** — you learn to hold both selves; many "never fully return to who they were."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"At first the returnee feels ___, but a second stage often brings ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 for the second stage.
- **Stretch:** The writer says "the discomfort is evidence of growth." Why might feeling uncomfortable be a good sign? · The title calls it "the shock nobody warns you about." Why is being unprepared part of what makes it hard?

### "How Tea Travelled the World"
- **Organiser — sequence / timeline (a journey through history).** Title: *The journey of tea.*
  - **c. 2737 BCE, China** — discovered by accident (leaves blow into an emperor's boiling water); kept secret for centuries.
  - **Along Asian routes** — monks carry it to Japan (a quiet ceremony); traders take it west on the Silk Road.
  - **1600s, Europe** — arrives by ship, a luxury for the rich; becomes a British habit.
  - **Then India** — Britain grows its own in Assam; India becomes one of the largest producers today.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Tea began in ___, reached Europe in the ___, and was later grown by Britain in ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 3 and 4 for Europe and India.
- **Stretch:** The writer says tea "started wars, built fortunes, and created customs." Why list these three very different effects together? · Which words in paragraph 1 tell you the writer is not sure the discovery story is true?

### "The First Six Months: A Newcomer's Field Notes"
- **Organiser — sequence / timeline (month by month).** Title: *Six months, six phases.*
  - **Month 1** — pure logistics: defeating a washing machine; everything takes three times as long.
  - **Month 2** — the "language wall"; carries a notebook, adds one phrase a day.
  - **Month 3** — neighbours start to talk; "a regular, not a visitor."
  - **Months 4–5 (hardest)** — novelty gone, home not yet felt; the "dip."
  - **Month 6** — stops translating street signs; the city is "a place I lived in."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"In month one everything took ___ times as long; by month six the writer had stopped ___."* Hint — look in the first and last paragraphs.
- **Stretch:** The writer says months 4–5 have "the shortest" notes. What does the length of the notes tell you about that time? · Why does the writer describe the strangeness turning "into the texture of an ordinary life" rather than just disappearing?

### "The Month I Stopped Translating"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: Before | both | After).** Title: *Before belonging / after belonging.*
  - **Before** — translated every sentence before speaking; the moment "had usually passed"; felt the bread was "the wrong shape."
  - **Both** — same street, same neighbours, same phrase book.
  - **After** — bought bread without rehearsing; laughed at a joke "before I had finished translating it."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"At first the writer ___ every sentence in their head; later they ___ without rehearsing."* Hint — look in paragraphs 1 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says belonging "arrives in pieces, on days you are not watching for it." Why describe it that way instead of as a single moment? · Dusk "always felt like an accusation." What does this unusual image show about how the writer felt early on?

## UNIT 5 · Art & Technology

### "Should a Machine Win an Art Prize?"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart), plus a middle column.** Title: *Should AI art win prizes?*
  - **For** — judge the result on merit alone · photography was once dismissed too · humans still choose prompts and edit.
  - **Against** — AI trained on unpaid artists' work → any prize is "borrowed labour" · perfect images in seconds may lower art's value · could damage the prize's credibility.
  - **Middle ground** — don't ban it, but label entries / give AI its own separate category.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Supporters say the picture should be judged on its ___; opponents say the prize is unfair because ___."* Hint — look in the "case against" paragraph for the fairness argument.
- **Stretch:** The writer calls training data "borrowed labour." Why is *borrowed* a stronger word here than *used*? · The writer ends by saying the question "will be asked again… and the one after that." What does this tell you about the writer's view of a final answer?

### "Where Does an AI Learn to Draw?"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *How an AI learns a style — and why artists object.*
  - company scrapes billions of images from the internet → **the program learns patterns of colour, shading, framing**
  - it can imitate a named artist "in the style of" → **the artist must have been studied in detail during training**
  - no attribution + no consent + no compensation → **many artists call it "a form of taking"**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"The program does not copy one picture; it learns general ___ from millions of ___."* Hint — look in the "Copying or learning?" section.
- **Stretch:** The writer repeats three words: *attribution, consent, compensation.* Why group the argument around three clear words? · The last line says "the rules are now struggling to catch up." What does the word *struggling* suggest about who is winning — the technology or the law?

### "Street Art: Vandalism or Public Gallery?"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart).** Title: *Street art: public gallery or property damage?*
  - **For** — makes art free for everyone, not just ticket-buyers · reflects the neighbourhood's real culture · can attract tourists.
  - **Against** — unauthorised work is legally property damage · the wall's owner should decide · a "famous" wall attracts poor-quality graffiti too.
  - **A middle path** — cities create "legal walls" where artists paint freely.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Supporters argue street art is ___ to everyone; critics argue it is still, legally, ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The title asks a question ("Vandalism or Public Gallery?"). Why open with a question rather than a statement? · Which single word in the passage shows the writer thinks skill does not settle the legal question?

### "The Changing Library"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: Old library | both | Modern library).** Title: *The library, then and now.*
  - **Old** — a quiet place to borrow a book and bring it back; "very little else."
  - **Both** — free · open to everyone · quiet study corners still exist.
  - **Modern** — free computers and internet · clubs and classes (story time, coding, languages) · lends tools, board games, even instruments.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"A library used to be simply a place to ___ books; today it also offers ___ and ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 for the new services.
- **Stretch:** The writer repeats that almost everything is "still completely free." Why return to the word *free* at the very end? · The writer calls the building "warmer and more sociable than it ever used to be." What attitude to the change do these words reveal?

### "The Thing a Machine Cannot Feel"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: The human artist | both | The machine).** Title: *A drawing of grandmother's hands.*
  - **Human illustrator** — remembers the kitchen smell, the afternoon she taught him; each choice "carries a meaning I put there on purpose."
  - **Both** — can make a beautiful image; can show the *look* of grief or joy.
  - **Machine** — "has no grandmother," "no afternoon to remember"; "intended nothing"; the feeling "you brought yourself."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"When the illustrator draws his grandmother's hands he is really drawing what he ___ about her; the machine ___ nothing by it."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says the machine's pictures "come from nowhere." Why is *nowhere* such a damaging word for art? · The writer admits many AI pictures are "stunning." Why include praise for the thing he is arguing against?

## UNIT 6 · Fashion & Identity

### "The Real Price of Cheap Clothes"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *The hidden cost of fast fashion.*
  - keep prices low → **clothes mass-produced by low-paid workers in unsafe factories**
  - clothes "designed to be temporary" → **we keep buying → mountains of waste, some taking centuries to break down**
  - buy fewer/better, choose second-hand, support fair brands → **the pressure on the industry shifts**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Fast fashion keeps prices low by ___, and the environmental cost includes ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says the cheapness is "partly paid for by someone else, somewhere we never see." Why keep that person invisible in the sentence, then name them? · The essay is balanced ("Cheap clothing is not simply evil"). How does admitting the good points make the argument stronger?

### "Why I Stopped Dressing for Other People"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: Before the coat | both | After the coat).** Title: *Dressing to disappear / dressing as myself.*
  - **Before** — wore the same neutral clothes as everyone; "blended in completely," felt "completely invisible."
  - **Both** — still not a bold dresser; still the same person underneath.
  - **After** — a green second-hand coat; chose things she liked; "stood a little straighter."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"For years the writer dressed to ___; after the green coat she began choosing clothes because ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 1 and 5.
- **Stretch:** The writer expected confidence to come first and bold clothes second, but "it was the other way around." Why is this reversal the heart of the piece? · The last line is only three words: "wear the coat." Why end so short?

### "How a Trend Travels"
- **Organiser — sequence (how a look spreads and fades).** Title: *The life of a trend.*
  1. Begins small — a runway show or a 30-second clip (most go nowhere).
  2. An influencer with millions of followers picks it up → everywhere within days.
  3. Example: a Seoul student's blazer video → 3 weeks later, near-copies for sale in Bangkok.
  4. It becomes mainstream and cheap → the starters have moved on → it feels "tired."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"A trend usually begins ___, then spreads fast when ___ shares it."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 for the Seoul example.
- **Stretch:** The writer says "the faster a trend rises, the faster it falls." Why put the two halves of the sentence in balance like that? · The final questions are about "how quickly we copy one another." What is the writer really inviting you to think about — clothes, or people?

### "What My Clothes Say About Me"
- **Organiser — compare (a 4-way grid; one attitude per person).** Title: *Four people, four reasons to dress.*
  - **Priya** — comfort and freedom; "getting dressed in ten seconds is a kind of freedom."
  - **Marcus** — quality over quantity; three jackets that last, not thirty that fall apart.
  - **Lena** — play and change; clothes are "how I try on different versions of myself."
  - **Sam** — belonging; watches the group and matches it — "I'm one of you."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"For Priya clothes mean ___, but for Sam they mean ___."* Hint — look at speakers A and D.
- **Stretch:** Marcus says "the expensive coat is the cheap one." How can an expensive thing be the cheap one? · Two speakers (Lena and Sam) want opposite things from clothes. Whose reason do you find more convincing, and which of their words shows their feeling most clearly?

### "The Grey Army"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart on the school uniform).** Title: *What the grey uniform does.*
  - **For / a kindness** — "a curtain I can hide behind"; on a bad morning "the same grey cannot be mocked"; makes students equal.
  - **Against / a lid** — "presses two thousand restless lives into one flat shape"; makes students "disappear"; sparks tiny rebellions (a bright lace, a hidden badge).
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"The writer says the uniform can feel like a ___ to hide behind, but on other mornings like a ___ pressing him down."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer calls two thousand students "a single grey animal breathing in and out at the bell." What does this image suggest about how individuals feel in a crowd? · The ending says "the colour leaking through." What is the "colour" really standing for?

## UNIT 7 · Sport & Esports

### "What Counts as a Sport?"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart: does esports pass the test?).** Title: *Esports against the four criteria.*
  - **Passes** — organised competition (leagues, referees, anti-cheat) · governing bodies (national federations) · skill (hours of daily training).
  - **Contested** — physical exertion: critics say sitting at a screen isn't bodily effort; supporters say fast hand movements and split-second reactions are physical too.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Officials weigh four criteria: physical exertion, skill, ___ and ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 1 for the list of four.
- **Stretch:** The writer says the argument is "where the argument is fought," not settled by "personal taste." Why does the writer prefer criteria to opinion? · Which criterion does the writer make the whole debate turn on, and why that one?

### "Bodies Pushed Hard"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes).** Title: *What a gamer's body goes through.*
  - hundreds of precise actions per minute for hours → **needs fine motor control and intense concentration**
  - years of rapid, repeated movements → **repetitive strain injuries that can end a career**
  - long hours seated + bright screen → **back, neck and eye problems**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Top players need fine motor control rather than ___ strength, and rapid movements can cause ___ injuries."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says a strain injury can end a career "as surely as a torn muscle ends a runner's." Why compare the gamer to a runner? · The writer says teams pay for physiotherapists "not… for show." What point is being made by pointing at where the money goes?

### "The Business Behind the Team"
- **Organiser — cause → effect / structure (arrow boxes: money in, people, then risk).** Title: *An esports team as a business.*
  - **Money in** — sponsorship (largest), prize money, streaming, tickets, merchandise.
  - **People** — head coach, analyst, team manager, sports psychologist, plus an academy for juniors.
  - **But the risk** — most retire in their mid-twenties; rosters change constantly; burnout ends careers early.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"The biggest source of money for most teams is ___, and the staff include a head coach and an ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says a top team "looks less like a group of friends gaming and more like a small sports business." Why open with that comparison? · Critics say "money alone proves nothing." How does the writer treat that objection — dismiss it or take it seriously?

### "The Hidden Work Behind a Marathon"
- **Organiser — sequence / cause → effect (the training idea).** Title: *The unseen months before a race.*
  - **The rule** — the body adapts to stress only if also given time to recover → weeks mix hard and easy runs.
  - **Long run** grows to ~32 km (never the full distance in training → injury risk).
  - **Carb-loading** in the final days → muscles store as much energy as possible.
  - **"The wall"** at ~30 km → runners break the race into small parts to cope.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Runners rarely run the full ___ in training, and just before the race they eat extra ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says most runners' goal "is not to win but simply to finish." Why is that an important point about this sport? · The last line says runners race "their own" clock. What does the writer mean, and why end there?

### "The Roaring Dark"
- **Organiser — cause → effect (arrow boxes: is this a spectator sport?).** Title: *An esports final as a spectacle.*
  - 18,000 phone lights + noise "like a physical thing" → **behaves exactly as sports crowds always have**
  - finals fill football stadiums; millions watch at home → **the audience is "measurable, vast and growing"**
  - fans paint faces, gasp as one, weep when heroes lose → **"the oldest spectacle in the world"**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Fans paint their faces, ___ as one and ___ when their heroes lose — just like any sports crowd."* Hint — look in paragraph 4.
- **Stretch:** The writer opens "The arena is dark, and then it isn't." Why start with darkness and a sudden change? · The writer admits this "does not prove that esports demands the body of an Olympic sprinter." Why concede that in a piece arguing esports is a sport?

## UNIT 8 · Space Exploration

### "Why do we look up?"
- **Organiser — cause → effect / reasons (arrow boxes: why we explore).** Title: *Reasons we go to space.*
  - ancient wonder → **farmers marked seasons, sailors found their way; curiosity "we cannot help"**
  - the 1969 Moon landing → **inspiration: children suddenly wanted to build spacecraft**
  - competition between rival nations → **space became a way of "demonstrating power" and prestige**
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"People explore space partly out of ___ and partly to show ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 1 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer says we explore "because we cannot help ourselves." What view of human nature does that suggest? · The passage admits missions "fail" and progress is "painfully slow." Why include the failures in a piece about wonder?

### "The Long Journey to Mars"
- **Organiser — sequence / cause → effect (three challenges).** Title: *Three problems on the way to Mars.*
  1. **Fuel** — a rocket must carry huge fuel → engineers send supplies ahead on uncrewed flights to save weight.
  2. **Radiation** — no magnetic-field protection → shields of water and plastic (lighter materials block it better than metal).
  3. **The human mind** — up to 3 years trapped together → space agencies run long isolation experiments on Earth.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"To save weight, engineers plan to send ___ ahead of the crew; to block radiation they test shields made of ___."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer ends "a question of when, not if." What attitude to the difficulties does that phrase reveal? · Engineers "cannot simply aim at where Mars is today." Why does the writer use this to show the reader how hard the task is?

### "What space gives back to Earth"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart on spin-offs).** Title: *Do space spin-offs justify the cost?*
  - **Supporters** — satellites give weather forecasts and navigation · space's harsh demands drove advances in materials and medicine · a space programme is "an advanced research laboratory."
  - **Critics** — accept spin-offs exist, but ask if space is the *cheapest* way · why not fund medicine and energy directly? · benefits are used "after the event to justify spending decided for other reasons."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"A spin-off is a product developed for ___ that ends up helping life on ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 1 for the definition.
- **Stretch:** Critics ask why not "fund medicine and energy directly." Why is this the strongest point against the supporters? · The writer says satellites are "reminders that looking outward has… changed the way we live down here." Whose side does that final line lean towards?

### "Where next — and should we go?"
- **Organiser — compare (a 4-voice grid; one view each).** Title: *Four voices on the future of space.*
  - **Engineer (Vance)** — a Moon base is feasible in 20 years; a Mars colony far harder; "be honest about what is possible."
  - **Economist (Liu)** — public money is limited; leave it to private investors, not taxpayers.
  - **Ethicist (Adeyemi)** — worries about fairness: will space benefit only the wealthy?
  - **Entrepreneur (Reyes)** — expansion is "inevitable"; caution is "a failure of nerve."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"The economist worries about ___, while the ethicist worries about ___."* Hint — look at voices [B] and [C].
- **Stretch:** The writer says "the technology… is the easy part" and the "harder questions are about what we value." Why put values above technology? · Reyes calls herself "a visionary" and dismisses caution. How does the writer's wording quietly invite you to doubt her?

### "Counting the cost"
- **Organiser — for | against (T-chart on space spending).** Title: *Is the money worth it?*
  - **Critics** — a crewed mission "can swallow billions" · opportunity cost: money not spent on hospitals or schools · launches pollute the atmosphere and fill orbit with debris.
  - **Supporters** — space gets only a tiny fraction of budgets (far less than defence) · the money is paid to engineers and universities on Earth, creating jobs and knowledge.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Critics point to the ___ cost — money not spent on hospitals; supporters reply the sums are a ___ fraction of budgets."* Hint — look in paragraphs 2 and 3.
- **Stretch:** The writer ends that defenders must win "a harder argument… than simply pointing at the stars." What does "pointing at the stars" stand for? · The passage lists money, danger *and* the environment as costs. Why does the writer refuse to let the argument be about money alone?

## UNIT 9 · Learning & Self-Teaching

### "Teaching Myself, One Tab at a Time"
- **Organiser — sequence / cause → effect (Maya's self-teaching).** Title: *Learning with no classroom.*
  1. No course nearby → she searches "how to animate for beginners."
  2. Thousands of free tutorials → she must *choose* which are worth her time (resourcefulness).
  3. Keeps a notebook of what went wrong → reflection "mattered more than any single tutorial."
  4. Learned alone → practised mistakes unnoticed → fixed it by posting work online for honest feedback.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"Being a self-directed learner meant more than watching videos; it meant ___ which ones were worth her time."* Hint — look in paragraph 2.
- **Stretch:** Maya says "no one told me I was doing it wrong… because no one was watching." What is the hidden danger of learning alone? · Her advice is "Pick something tiny. Finish it." Why does she say learning is "less about talent" than about a "quiet decision"?

### "The Most Useful Thing You'll Get Wrong"
- **Organiser — sequence (three steps for learning from a mistake).** Title: *Turning an error into information.*
  1. **Look** — resist moving on; work out exactly where and why your thinking went astray.
  2. **Invite other eyes** — honest feedback reveals a weakness you keep walking past.
  3. **Reflect** — make a deliberate plan; many keep a record so patterns become visible.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"For fast learners a mistake is not a verdict on their ability; it is ___."* Hint — look in paragraph 1 (the last word).
- **Stretch:** The writer says "a mistake you hide is a mistake you cannot learn from." Why is hiding a mistake worse than making it? · The essay uses a baby learning to walk. Why choose that example to change how you feel about failing?

### "You Will Never Finish Learning — and That's the Point"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: The old world of work | both | Today).** Title: *A finish line that no longer exists.*
  - **Old world** — learn a trade once, cross the "finish line," coast for life.
  - **Both** — your education still matters (a "foundation").
  - **Today** — skills have "a shelf life"; the key skill is "the ability to learn quickly… and begin again without shame."
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"There used to be a ___ line in education; today skills have a ___ life."* Hint — look in paragraphs 1 and 2.
- **Stretch:** The writer reframes a threat as "an open door for the person who is willing." Why turn the frightening idea into a hopeful one? · The title says "and that's the point." What does the writer want you to celebrate rather than fear?

### "The Saturday I Didn't Want"
- **Organiser — compare (double-bubble: Before / after the repair café).** Title: *What the writer learned at Mill Street.*
  - **Before** — went only to avoid an argument; expected "a draughty hall" and "pretending to be useful."
  - **Both** — the café does save people money.
  - **After** — sees it is really about the "feeling of being capable" and company; now says when he's stuck and lets someone older show him.
- **Support:** Sentence-starter — *"The café helps people ___ things themselves rather than mending the items for them."* Hint — look in paragraph 2 (Femi's rule).
- **Stretch:** Femi says "If they fix it with us, they don't [come back]." Why is teaching better than fixing, in his view? · The writer says the money "turned out to be the least interesting thing about it." What did he find more interesting, and which words show it?

### "The People Who Helped Me Learn"
- **Organiser — compare (a 4-voice grid).** Title: *Four learners, four communities.*
  - **Tomas** — an online coding forum; answering others "taught me more than any tutorial."
