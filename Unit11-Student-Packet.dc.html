Three routines to remember
  • Clue vs guess: a clue is a fact you can point to; a theory is an idea about the facts. Keep them apart.
  • Test the claim: ask who says so? · can it be checked? · is it too neat?
  • The Skill Bank: one page in your notebook that grows every lesson — your own revision sheet.

1 · Vocabulary reference

The unit's words, grouped by strand. Say them, then use them in your writing and speaking.

Investigation
alibi — proof you were elsewhere when a crime happened
clue — a piece of information that helps solve a mystery
evidence — facts or signs that show whether something is true
examine — to look at something carefully and in detail
investigate — to try carefully to find out the truth
motive — a reason for doing something, especially a crime
suspect — a person thought to be guilty of a crime
trace — a small sign that shows something happened
witness — a person who saw something happen
Reasoning
assume — to think something is true without proof
coincidence — when things happen at the same time by chance
conclude — to decide something after considering the facts
deduce — to work something out from the facts you have
doubt — a feeling of not being sure about something
evident — clear and easy to see or understand
prove — to show with evidence that something is true
theory — an idea that tries to explain why something happens
verdict — an official decision about guilt
Strangeness
baffling — impossible to understand; very confusing
bizarre — very strange and unusual
eerie — strange in a way that makes you feel afraid
inexplicable — impossible to explain
mysterious — strange, and difficult to explain
puzzling — confusing and difficult to explain
sinister — seeming evil or threatening
uncanny — strange in an unsettling, unnatural way
Reveal & conceal (hoax or real?)
conceal — to hide something carefully
confess — to admit that you have done something wrong
conspiracy — a secret plan by a group to do harm
deny — to say that something is not true
hoax — a trick that makes people believe something untrue
reveal — to make known something that was hidden
suspicious — making you feel that something is wrong
uncover — to discover something hidden or secret
vanish — to disappear suddenly and completely
Events & fear — for the story & report
disappearance — the act of going missing
dread — a strong feeling of fear about what may happen
legend — an old, popular story that may not be true
rumour — a story passed on that may not be true
suspense — a feeling of excited or anxious uncertainty
testimony — a formal statement that something is true
unexplained — without any known reason or cause
unreliable — not able to be trusted or believed
Reasoning chunks — for the report & discussion
the evidence suggests — base a claim on facts
it seems likely that — a careful, hedged guess
there's no proof that — flag a claim with no evidence
must / might / can't have — weigh how sure you are
we may never know — accept an open ending
Word families — learn one, gain three

One root grows into several. Learn the family and you always have the right form.

investigate → investigation → investigator
deduce → deduction → deductive
conceal → concealment → concealed
First-language bridge

In your first language, note what these key ideas mean — it helps them stick: evidence · deduce · hoax.

5 · Grammar (Use of English)

5a · Narrative tenses (tell the story)

Past simple for the main events; past continuous for the background; past perfect (had done) for what happened earlier.

  1. Choose the tense: "By the time the boat arrived, the keepers (vanish) ______."
  2. Add background with past continuous: "She ___ at the window when she saw the van."
  3. Write your own sentence using had + past participle for an earlier event.
5b · Past deduction (weigh the clues)

Match the modal to how sure you are: must have (sure) · might/could have (possible) · can't have (sure not) + past participle.

  1. "The lock had no scratch, so the thief ___ have known the code."
  2. "His ticket was upstairs, so he ___ have been in the study."
  3. Write one deduction sentence about a clue of your own.

6 · Writing

6a · A mystery story (150–200 words)
Shape (3 beats): Set-up (scene + you lock the door) → Strange event (the open door + one detail that can't be explained; slow the moment) → Reflection (how you felt; end on a question).   Include the door you locked, later found open. Suspense move + controlled tenses (had locked · was falling); target words (eerie · uncanny · vanished · baffling · inexplicable).
6b · A report on a strange event (150–200 words)
Shape (4 headings): Introduction → What people claim (attributed) → How reliable it is (weigh; one ordinary explanation) → Conclusion (don't overclaim).   Neutral, formal voice; attribute every claim (residents report…); past-deduction modals (might/can't/must have); target words (witness · testimony · unreliable · evidence · conclude).
