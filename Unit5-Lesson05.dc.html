Where Does an AI Learn to Draw?

Read · then read again

The training problem. An AI image program is not born knowing how to draw. It learns by example — and the examples are real pictures made by real people. To build one, a company collects an enormous number of images, often using software to scrape them automatically from across the internet. Billions of images are gathered, usually without anyone asking the people who made them.

Learning a style. From all this material, the program learns patterns — how an artist uses colour, how a comic shades a face, how a photographer frames a street. It can later imitate a distinctive style on demand. Ask for a picture "in the style of" a particular illustrator, and the result can be uncannily close — even though the company may never name whose work it used.

Copying or learning? Companies argue the program does not store or copy any single picture; it learns general patterns, the way a student learns from the masters. The output, they say, is new — not derivative. Many artists disagree: the program could not produce anything without the millions of works it absorbed. To them, learning a living person's style without consent is a form of taking.

The fairness question. Three words come up again and again. Attribution: the original artist is almost never credited. Consent: the artists were not asked. Compensation: even when companies profit, the artists whose work trained the program are usually paid nothing. Some call it exploitation; others say it may count as fair use — a question the courts have only begun to decide.
